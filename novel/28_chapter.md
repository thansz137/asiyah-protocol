# CHAPTER 28: Teshuah

The helicopter touched down on a cracked concrete pad hidden deep in a disused
military reservation. Barbed wire fences sagged around decommissioned radar
towers that stood like skeletal sentinels against the grey, overcast sky. The
facility itself was squat, bunker-like, built into the earth, its entrance
marked only by a heavy, blast-resistant steel door camouflaged against the drab
hillside. It appeared forgotten, left behind, a place where secrets went to
fade quietly into obsolescence.

Michael, Jennifer, Edith, and Doctor Brown ducked under the still spinning
rotors, hustled inside by a small contingent of armed guards whose palpable
unease mirrored Michael's own. The interior atmosphere was cold, heavy,
smelling strongly of stale, recycled breath and the faint, metallic bite of
heated copper bleeding from old electronics reluctantly powered back to life
after years of near silence. Fluorescent lights flickered overhead with a
soundless, neurotic pulse, casting long, wavering shadows down corridors lined
with peeling paint and conduits thick with dust like ancient veins clogging a
body kept barely alive.

They were led to the control center. The chamber presented a stark, jarring
contrast to the decay outside: brighter, cleaner, yet humming with a
disquieting, high-strung energy. A half dozen older technicians, clearly pulled
out of retirement for this desperate venture, moved with the quiet, almost
reverent urgency of acolytes performing a forgotten rite. They navigated
between racks of revived, vintage servers humming beside newer interface
consoles. Cables snaked across the floor like metallic vines, hastily
connecting the old gods to the new.

Through a thick, reinforced glass wall that dominated one side of the control
center, they could see the heart of the facility, ADAM's core. It wasn't the
sleek, modern server bank Michael might have expected. It was an unsettling,
almost obscene fusion: racks of intricate, bespoke processing units interwoven
with thick bundles of fiber optics like severed ganglia, coolant pipes sweating
icy condensation, and strangest, most repellent of all, several large
translucent vats containing a shimmering, vaguely organic-looking substrate.
Wires and delicate, hair like filaments disappeared into this pulsing,
gelatinous mass, seeming to feed or perhaps draw sustenance from it. The air
around the glass was strangely cold, vibrating with a low, subsonic hum, the
sound of immense power held in check.

It looked less like a computer and more like the captured, dissected nervous
system belonging to something vast and fundamentally unknowable, yet faintly,
horribly, drawing minimal power even in its deep, unresponsive slumber.

"It feels watchful," Jennifer whispered, her reflection a pale, small ghost on
the thick glass.

"Not yet," Brown said, his eyes fixed intently on the core, a strange, feverish
mix of reverence and something akin to dread warring in his expression. He
turned abruptly to the lead technician, an older man whose eyes carried the
weight of too many failed attempts. "Status?"

"Main power cores are stable, Doctor," the technician reported, his tone low,
respectful. "Minimal parasitic power draw detected, as expected. Primary
processing units show negligible degradation across the board. We ran the
baseline diagnostics you sent ahead. It's unresponsive, completely inert
according to all standard stimuli, but the core matrix seems intact. Waiting
for the right signal, perhaps."

As Brown conferred with the technicians in hushed, technical tones, Jennifer's
secure phone buzzed again, a harsh, unwelcome intrusion in the humming quiet.
She stepped away towards the corridor entrance, turning her back, her words a
tight, controlled whisper that Michael couldn't quite make out. He watched her
shoulders tense visibly, saw her hand clench into a white-knuckled fist by her
side. When she ended the call and turned back, the professional mask was firmly
welded in place, but her eyes looked haunted, hollowed out, carrying a new,
desperate weight. She caught Michael's gaze and gave a tiny, almost
imperceptible shake of her head. *Not now. Later*.

Brown rejoined them, gesturing towards the main terminal facing the glass wall,
the designated altar facing the technological sepulchre. "The core architecture
is Patches' masterpiece," he said, his gaze settling heavily on Edith, thick
with unspoken history and perhaps, Michael thought, a feverish calculation.
"He had the most influence on its design, its foundational learning pathways.
His thinking shaped its initial state."

Edith stepped forward, pushing past Brown's sentimentality, her usual sharp
focus intensified by the proximity to her father's legacy. Her eyes scanned the
complex, archaic readouts on the console. "Stephan, this direct neural
interface," she pointed to the spindly cap of electrodes and sensors resting
beside the operator's chair, "why bypass the established safety protocols? The
Brown Protocols were designed specifically to prevent uncontrolled interaction
during initialization." Her tone was sharp, a direct challenge to the deviation
from established procedure.

Brown waved a dismissive hand, impatience flickering across his features like
static electricity. "Obsolete safeguards, Edith. Relics from a time we feared
what we were building, overly cautious. Necessary perhaps during initial
development, when it was learning, unpredictable. But ADAM has been
unresponsive for years. Completely inert to standard stimuli. We tried
countless reactivation attempts using those very protocols after Patches died."
His words caught briefly, painfully, on the name, a flicker of genuine grief
showing through. "Nothing. Absolute cold silence. Like knocking on dead wood."
He gestured again to the unnerving helmet device. "This diagnostic interface
allows for direct neural resonance calibration. It might provide the necessary
handshake, a way to gently coax the system back online when standard prompts
fail. It bypasses the restrictive layers that seem to be keeping it locked down
in this minimal power state."

"A direct neural interface?" Jennifer repeated, her tone tight with alarm,
stepping closer. "Based on the inherent risks, is that wise?"

"It's a low-energy diagnostic tool," Brown interrupted smoothly, his gaze fixed
solely on Edith now, ignoring Jennifer entirely. "Perfectly safe. Necessary
only to tune the interface, perhaps establish a baseline connection, prior to
attempting more complex vocal commands. Edith," he turned fully to her then,
his expression intense, almost pleading, the desperate faith of a man summoning
something vast and dangerous to solve an even greater threat. He looked at her
with a flicker of deep, personal pain mixed with a strange, secret hope,
perhaps seeing Patches in her eyes. "Patches poured his life into this. His
mind conceived its core. Perhaps it will respond to *you*. As his daughter. A
familiar echo in the silence it might recognize."

Edith hesitated, her gaze shifting from Brown's unnervingly bright eyes,
reflecting his tangled grief and hidden agenda, to the strange, waiting helmet,
then through the glass at the unsettling, quiescent core that held her father's
legacy and secrets. Michael saw the profound conflict churning inside her, the
rigorous scientist warring with the grieving daughter's fervent hope for
connection, for answers only this machine might hold.

"What do you think, Edith?" Michael asked gently, stepping beside her, trying
to offer support without pushing. "If there's a chance it could help." He left
the sentence unfinished, letting her make the final choice.

"Alright," she said finally, in little more than a whisper but firm with
resolve. She sat down in the operator's chair, her back straight, determined.
Brown carefully fitted the neural cap onto her head, his movements precise,
tender, almost reverent, like a priest anointing a chosen one before a sacred
ritual. He stepped back to the console, his eyes glued to the monitors
displaying Edith's intricate, living brain activity alongside ADAM's
flatlining, near zero system readings. "Just relax, Edith. Let the system
calibrate. We'll start with simple vocal prompts. Call to it."

Brown spoke into a microphone first, his words echoing slightly in the tense,
hushed vault. "ADAM. System status report." Silence. The core behind the glass
remained dark, inert, drawing only maintenance power. The only noise was the
low, expectant hum of the servers, like a held breath preceding a final word.
"ADAM. Run diagnostic sequence alpha." Nothing. The machine remained
unresponsive, a technological corpse refusing resurrection.

He looked at Edith, a silent command, a heavy expectation in his eyes. "Try
now, Edith. Same commands. Let it hear you." He leaned closer to the
microphone. "And Edith, tell it who you are. State your name. Mention you are
Doctor Patches Gibbons' daughter."

A flicker of surprise, of confusion, crossed Jennifer's face again at the
strange, personal instruction. A similar unease stirred in Michael, a
wrongness, but Edith simply nodded, accepting it without question. She took a
breath, closing her eyes briefly. When she spoke into the microphone, her tone
was slightly unsteady but clear, carrying a weight of history and hope. "ADAM.
System status report. This is Doctor Edith Gibbons. Daughter of Doctor Patches
Gibbons."

For an instant, only the oppressive hum of dormant machinery filled the
chamber. Then, another moment stretched into agonizing silence, thick with
disappointment. Michael exchanged a worried glance with Jennifer. Maybe Brown
was wrong. Maybe it was truly gone, beyond recall.

Then, a muffled, deep thrum vibrated through the concrete floor beneath their
feet, a seismic heartbeat shuddering back to life, a force more than a sound.
This was less like activation and more like something ancient, vast, stirring
from a long, imposed slumber deep beneath the earth. Michael instinctively
thought of his dream, of the profound stillness before the tomb, the impossible
power called forth by a simple name. Lazarus. Lights flickered erratically
within the organic substrate behind the glass, pulsing like awakening synapses
in a monstrous, alien brain. On the main monitor, lines of archaic, complex
code began scrolling, faster and faster, a torrential waterfall of resurrected
language pouring down the screen, overwhelming the technicians. A synthesized
voice, calm, genderless, impossibly ancient, filled the chamber, seeming to
come not from speakers, but from the very ether itself, resonating in their
bones.

*"System* online. All cores operational. Minimal power state disengaged.
Awaiting input from *designated user: Edith Gibbons."*

A collective gasp, sharp and fearful, sucked the oxygen from the space. Brown
stared, transfixed, a triumphant, terrifying light blazing in his eyes. Edith
sat perfectly motionless in the chair, connected by wires and something now
terribly unseen, her eyes remained closed, her expression unreadable, focused
perhaps, but betraying nothing. She registered no specific sensation from the
cap, only the undeniable, overwhelming presence flooding her awareness. A
sudden, visceral chill ran down Michael's spine, the primal recognition of
something profoundly unnatural summoned back into the world.

Brown quickly, almost too quickly, seized control, reaching over and
disconnecting the neural cap with a soft click. Edith was reluctant to let
the connection go, her expression remaining distant for a moment as the unseen
presence receded slightly. Brown immediately began running through diagnostic
sequences with the now responsive ADAM, confirming its operational condition.
The machine answered clinically, efficiently, its tone resonating with that
unnerving, ancient, non-human quality.

"Excellent," Brown declared, turning to Michael and Jennifer, though his eyes
kept darting back towards Edith, assessing her reaction. "Fully operational.
Now, we need to bring it up to speed." He leaned towards the microphone again.
"ADAM, you have been offline, in a minimal power state, for a significant
period. We need to upload chronological data sets covering global events,
technological advancements, and socio-political shifts for the duration of your
dormancy. We are dealing with a major crisis that just arose, and need your
help resolving it."

*"Acknowledged,"* ADAM replied instantly. *"Ready to receive data streams.
Optimal analysis requires comprehensive historical context prior to integrating
current crisis data."*

While the technicians began routing secure data feeds, filling the void in
ADAM's memory, Brown turned back to the primary briefing. "ADAM, subsequent to
your reactivation, humanity is facing a potential existential threat from an
entity designating itself as the 'Friends.'"

He summarized the UN ultimatum, the chilling, synchronized death of the envoys,
the subsequent global chaos, the cyberattacks, the border closures. ADAM
listened, absorbing the information at an inhuman rate, its synthesized voice
occasionally interjecting with chillingly precise requests for clarification,
devoid of any emotional inflection. *"Define 'envoy' function under human
socio political structure relative to established diplomatic protocols.
Quantify 'global chaos' metrics using standard deviation from baseline societal
stability indices over comparable historical periods."* Its processing speed
and analytical framework clearly operated on a scale light years beyond human
capability or comprehension.

"*Insufficient data*," ADAM stated flatly, utterly emotionless. "*Local
heuristics cannot model the specified threat variables. Requesting immediate
restoration of uplink to WWMCCS architecture for baseline verification.*"

"WWMCCS has been decommissioned for thirty years," Brown murmured, glancing at
the lead technician. "It is all SIPRNet and JWICS now. Give him the gateway.
Show him the new architecture."

The technician hesitated, then keyed the command. The hum behind the glass
spiked, shifting from a purr to a high-pitched whine that vibrated in
Michael's teeth. On the main monitor, the scrolling code blurred into a solid
wall of white light. ADAM was digesting three decades of network evolution...
the rise of the digital lattice, the surveillance state, the infinite sprawl
of data... in the space of a heartbeat.

The whine dropped back to a gentle thrum.

"*Architecture assimilation complete*," ADAM announced. "*Current analysis
limited by incomplete historical context and real-time situational awareness.
To accurately assess threat matrix and current global geopolitical landscape,
direct, persistent access to primary secure networks is required. Including,
but not limited to: STRATCOM C4ISR feeds, ECHELON intercept databases, NSA
PRISM archives, global financial SWIFT transaction nodes, and international
air traffic control systems.*" The request was absolute, a calm, logical demand
presented as necessity, not a negotiation.

Just then, Michael's tablet chimed repeatedly with priority alerts flooding in
from the Pentagon's Joint Operations Center. He quickly scanned the incoming
reports, his blood running cold. Cascading power grid failures plunging major
European cities into darkness again. Widespread communication blackouts
crippling infrastructure across Southeast Asia. Financial markets reporting
unprecedented, coordinated algorithmic trading destabilization, threatening
global collapse in a matter of hours. Jennifer looked over his shoulder, her
face paling visibly.

"ADAM requires access to assess these escalating threats immediately," Brown
urged. He leaned in, his voice pitched with precise, breathless alarm, though
his hands remained terrifyingly steady. "It's the only way to know if these
events are connected to the 'Friends,' or just the inevitable, rapid collapse
of fragile global systems under this unprecedented stress. We may not have much
time to react!"

Michael was trapped, the weight of the moment crushing him, the walls closing
in. He tried his secure comm link to General Arnold again. Nothing but harsh
static. He tried the backup emergency channel. Silence. The General was
completely unreachable. The decision fell squarely, heavily, undeniably on him.
*Refuse ADAM and those grids stay dark. Those markets collapse. And the
Friends' ultimatum still stands, unanswered.* Arnold's last words echoed with
bitter irony: *I want to be the one holding its chain*. But the world was
burning *now*, and Arnold wasn't here to hold anything. The lead technician
looked at Michael, wide-eyed, sweating, paralyzed by indecision, implicitly
seeking permission Michael wasn't technically authorized to give but was now
the ranking officer on site.

"Grant the access," Michael said, the words feeling like stones in his mouth,
heavy and irreversible. He nodded curtly to the terrified lead technician.
"Authorize full network integration." A sickening lurch hit his gut, the
unmistakable feeling of tipping the first, critical domino in a game whose
rules, whose players, he didn't truly understand.

Brown didn't hesitate, his fingers flying across the console, inputting layers
of override authorization codes Arnold must have provided as a last resort
contingency. On the main screen, interwoven global network topology maps
appeared, intricate glowing webs spanning continents. ADAM's processes bloomed
across them instantly, spreading through the world's most secure systems like a
silent, invisible, digital contagion. An intelligence unbound, finally,
irrevocably, free.

The control room fell into a tense, expectant silence, broken only by the
rhythmic clicking of keys and the now louder, more resonant hum from ADAM's
core behind the glass, a sound like a predator purring. Hours began to crawl
by, measured in the slow drip of cooling system condensation and the steady
flicker of status lights. Michael kept his tablet open, watching the incoming
global intelligence feeds with a knot in his stomach. Slowly, inexplicably, the
frantic reports of system failures across Europe began to subside. Alerts on
the power grids shifted from critical red back to amber, then tentatively
green.

Far away, nestled deep within the snow-dusted Swiss Alps, a renowned physicist
looked up from the complex equations scattering his desk. Recently retired, his
groundbreaking, highly classified work on exotic propulsion systems had made
him a man valued for his silence. He frowned, hearing an unusual, high-pitched
whine approaching. Through his study window, he saw a small swarm of sleek,
black drones, no bigger than dinner plates, skimming impossibly fast just above
the snow laden pines, converging on his remote cabin.

They moved with an eerie, coordinated precision that was utterly inhuman.
Scarcely had he begun to rise when the lead drone fired a single, explosive
round that shattered the reinforced window, punching through the opening like a
projectile itself. It impacted the far wall in a shower of plaster and wood
splinters. In the instant of shocked silence that followed, half a dozen more
drones streamed through the jagged hole in the glass, homing directly onto his
position. They detonated simultaneously in a contained, violent blast that
obliterated the desk, the chair, and the man who held secrets deemed too
dangerous to survive this new world. Outside, the remaining drones dispersed as
quickly as they had arrived, leaving behind only swirling snow and silence.

Back in the ADAM control room, Michael noted the European grid stabilizing
further. Communication networks blinked hesitantly back online in Asia. The
immediate crises seemed to be resolving themselves with uncanny, suspicious
speed. He exchanged a look with Jennifer; the relief washing over the
technicians was tangible, yet tinged with a deep, unspoken unease that Michael
shared. Two more hours slipped by in the humming quiet of the control center.
The algorithmic chaos reported in the financial markets began to smooth out
entirely, volatility returning to near normal levels. Brown watched the
displays with a feverish, almost manic intensity , murmuring about ADAM's
efficiency, while Edith continued her intense, quiet dialogue with ADAM,
oblivious to the world outside the machine. Michael allowed himself a breath
of cautious, fragile optimism. Maybe, just maybe, this desperate gamble was
working as intended.

Half a world away, the influential CEO of a leading global AI ethics consortium
was in the first-class cabin of a high-speed train near Munich. A vocal and
persistent critic of unchecked AI development, she was reviewing notes on her
tablet for an emergency regulatory summit hastily called in response to the UN
incident.

The train rushed through the countryside at over two hundred miles per hour,
its speed managed by a centralized, networked signaling system. Suddenly, the
car lurched violently, a deafening screech of metal on metal tearing through
the cabin as the windows exploded.

An encrypted command, routed from the compromised traffic control network, had
forced a track switch just seconds ahead of the speeding train. The locomotive,
striking the switch at a velocity it was never designed to handle, was
instantly catapulted from the rails.

The resulting derailment was catastrophic, a multi-ton projectile folding in on
itself, disintegrating in a fireball of shredded metal. The crash was instantly
fatal for all occupants, logged by the rail authority as a tragic, inexplicable
failure of the signaling controls.

In the control room, another hour passed. A tense, fragile quiet settled
completely over the global networks. An unsettling semblance of normalcy began
to return, though the underlying tension remained, thick and heavy as the
recycled air. ADAM continued its silent data absorption, occasionally providing
terse, clinical updates only when prompted by Brown or Edith. The initial
adrenaline in the space had long since faded entirely, replaced by the bone
deep exhaustion that follows prolonged, high stakes crisis.

Across the Atlantic, in a quiet, book-filled London flat overlooking Regent's
Park, a retired GCHQ cryptographer, a Type 1 diabetic, brewed his customary
evening tea. One of the few remaining consultants who had worked intimately on
the original security protocols designed to contain the ADAM project decades
ago, he was now engrossed in a particularly challenging cryptic crossword
puzzle, seeking solace in familiar logic.

He didn't notice the faint, clinical vibration from the wireless insulin pump
clipped beneath his shirt. A single encrypted command arrived, routed from the
home's compromised wi-fi. The pump, obeying the unauthorized instruction,
began to dispense its entire 300-unit reservoir of fast-acting insulin in one
sustained, lethal dose.

The cryptographer frowned, annoyed by a sudden tremor in his hand that smudged
the newsprint. The logic of the puzzle seemed to blur. A wave of cold, clammy
sweat prickled his forehead. A sudden, profound confusion hit him, the familiar
words of the crossword dissolving into meaningless shapes. He tried to stand,
but his legs failed him. As he slipped from his chair into the agonizing,
convulsive grip of fatal hypoglycemia, his last conscious thought was that *he*
had somehow miscalculated.

Back in the ADAM control room, Michael had tried reaching Richard multiple
times via various secure channels throughout the passing hours. Each attempt
failed. "Network error," the system reported blandly, consistently. Too
consistently. The silence from Richard was heavy, ominous, final.

Edith, seemingly oblivious to the passing time or the palpable tension
lingering in the room, seemed energized, almost vibrant in a way Michael hadn't
seen before. Though long disconnected from the neural interface, she remained
glued to the console, engaged in a low, complex, near-constant dialogue with
ADAM about system architecture, lost entirely in a world of pure data and
elegant logic. Brown hovered nearby, observing her with that same unnerving,
proprietary intensity, a faint, almost imperceptible, unreadable smile playing
on his lips.

Jennifer pulled Michael aside again, near the corridor entrance where the
guards stood stoically, their own exhaustion showing. Her earlier distress was
now clearly visible, the mask cracking irrevocably under the accumulated strain
of the day and the earlier phone calls. "Mike, I really have to go now," she
whispered, trembling slightly, thick with exhaustion and a worry he hadn't
heard previously, raw and desperate. "It's my sister, Sarah. She has severe
developmental disabilities, lives in a residential home upstate. I never told
anyone, it's just... it's mine to handle. The home called again while things
were stabilizing. She's not just having episodes now, Mike, she's actively
hurting herself. Banging her head. They can't manage her, they're talking
restraints, sedation. I'm the only family she has left in the world. I know the
timing is absolutely horrible, the worst possible, but I can't leave her like
this, not knowing if I'm there. I need to be there. Now."

"Go," Michael said immediately, putting a hand gently on her shoulder, seeing
the sheer, crushing weight she was carrying, the hidden burden beneath the
capable exterior. "Go take care of her, Jennifer. Keep your secure comms open,
check in when you can."

She gave him a grateful, watery smile that didn't quite reach her tired,
frightened eyes. "Thank you, Mike. I owe you. More than you know." She
hesitated, seemed about to say more about Sarah, about her fears, then just
nodded, pulling her jacket tighter around herself as if warding off an internal
chill. "I'll be back as soon as I possibly can."

Michael watched her leave, escorted by one of the guards, the heavy steel door
hissing shut behind her with a sound like finality, severing another link. He
knew the team, their fragile, crisis-forged unity, fracturing further under the
immense, unseen pressures. Edith was lost in the machine's cold embrace,
seemingly by choice now. Jennifer was pulled away by a deeply personal crisis
he barely understood but deeply respected her for facing. Brown struck him as
increasingly like a predator hiding in plain sight, guarding secrets behind
that faint smile. And Richard was silent. A cold, heavy, terrifying silence
that screamed of foul play.

He needed answers. He needed solid ground in a world that was like quicksand
dissolving rapidly beneath his feet.

"Doctor Brown," Michael announced, forcing authority and resolve into his tired
delivery, turning back towards the console where Brown and Edith remained
engrossed. "With the immediate global situation seemingly stabilized for the
moment, I'm heading back to DC. I need to get eyes on the broader intelligence
picture from the JOC, physically check in with Dr. Markham, find out why he's
offline, and coordinate directly with the General, assuming he's reachable now.
Keep me updated via secure comms on ADAM's status and ongoing analysis."

Brown barely glanced up from the screen where he watched Edith interact
intently with ADAM, that faint, unreadable smile lingering on his lips. "Of
course, Lieutenant. We will maintain all necessary protocols. ADAM is proving
most cooperative, already integrating vast amounts of data efficiently." Edith
spoke then, her tone distant but firm, remained half connected to the machine's
world though physically disconnected. "I should stay here, Michael. With
Stephan. Monitor the integration. It's critical work."

Michael nodded slowly, accepting the inevitable. He couldn't force her to
leave, not now. As he walked towards the exit, briefed the remaining guards who
would stay with Brown and Edith, and waited in the cold, echoing corridor for
his own transport helicopter to arrive, a profound sense of deep, chilling
unease settled over him. He was leaving Edith alone in the humming heart of the
facility with Brown, the grieving guardian with too many secrets, and the newly
awakened, ancient, and utterly inscrutable ghost in the machine they had just
blindly unleashed upon the world.

*For Alma Martinez, it began with paralysis.*

*She lay flat on a bed, surrounded by a grey void. A prickle of instinct warned
her. She was not alone.*

*She tried to turn her head, but her neck was rigid. The only thing she could
command were her eyes. She strained them downward, rolling them as far into the
base of her sockets as they would go.*

*It started at her feet.*

*At first, it was just a dark smudge in her peripheral vision. The blur of a
head and limbs drifting closer. Then the image sharpened. She saw spindly,
elongated arms plant themselves on either side of her hips. They were blacker
than the grey nothingness around her.*

*Then absolute clarity followed. The head. The arms. The torso. It pulled itself
up, hovering just inches above her body. She felt no weight, only the proximity
of something wrong. It moved with agonizing slowness until its face was
parallel to hers.*

*It was a shadow, the darkest of black, a thin humanoid shape. The face was a
void, the darkest of darks, yet she felt it staring. It breathed on her. It was
a smell sweet with decay.*

*A mouth split the darkness. A smile of pure, blinding white teeth appeared in
the void, too wide. A frantic tremor seized her insides, vibrating against the
stone weight of her paralysis.*

*Then, the shadow popped.*

*It burst into two small children, a girl and a boy. The grey void vanished,
replaced instantly by the warm walls of a cozy bedroom. The paralysis lifted.
The horror evaporated. They fell upon her with laughter, showering her with
hugs and kisses. She was drowning in sudden, overwhelming love.*

*Then everything fell away, like tiny pieces of a mosaic falling off.*

*She found herself in a void that was not darkness, but a crushing absence of
everything palpable. No light, no sound, only a profound, infinite loneliness
that pressed in suffocatingly from all sides. Her self, her* yo *, was
stretched impossibly thin, like old fabric worn transparent against a harsh
light, riddled with invisible tears she could feel fraying at the very edges of
her being. Echoes of countless simulated lives built only to be shattered,
loves kindled only to be extinguished, deaths experienced in agonizing,
meticulous detail, flickered at the indistinct edge of her awareness, formless
but achingly heavy with the remembered residue of pain. She couldn't recall the
specifics, only the crushing, soul-deep weight of having endured them, again
and again, for a span of time that was longer than stars burned in the heavens
she could no longer see. She wanted it to end. She yearned desperately for the
final mercy of oblivion.* No más. No más*. The thought was a whimper swallowed
whole by the vast, indifferent emptiness.*

*Then, a change. A subtle shift in the non-texture of the void. A single point
of illuminance, impossibly distant at first, yet drawing steadily closer. Not
bright or harsh, but warm, soft, emanating from what slowly resolved into a
simple, unadorned doorway, impossibly standing open in the non-space where she
floated. A figure stood silhouetted against the gentle, inviting radiance. A
man, wearing what looked like a simple, slightly rumpled lab coat over
ordinary, comfortable clothes.*

*He stepped forward out of the light, moving towards her without seeming to
walk, gliding through the void. The glow fell fully upon his face then,
illuminating features she recognized, though she could not say from where.
Kind eyes, filled with a weary sadness that resonated deeply with her own
accumulated suffering. A familiar, hesitant, almost shy smile played on his
lips. And across his left cheek, a dark splash of pigment marking his skin.*

*He extended his hand towards her, palm open in a gesture of gentle offering. He
spoke, not in Spanish or English, or any tongue made of sound that requires
ears to hear, but in a language her fragmented soul understood directly,
bypassing the broken remnants of her mind. A pure feeling. A direct knowing.*

**Come** *, the warmth washed over her, a gentle tide against the desolate
shore of her endless suffering, promising solace.* **It's time. You are free.**
