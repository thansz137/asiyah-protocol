# CHAPTER 23: Pilgrims

The old man listened to the static.

The radio sat on the kitchen counter, a dented gray box that hummed and cracked
through the night. The neighbors called him Noah Goldstein. But some spoke of
him in a quieter way, with lowered voices and glances toward the ground. They
said he might be one of the Lamed Vav Tzadikim. One of the thirty-six hidden
righteous souls whose existence, it was whispered, kept the world from
collapsing into ruin. It was folklore. But in his neighborhood, folklore had
teeth.

Noah never corrected them. He only listened.

Outside, snow fell in thick flakes. Pressing itself against the glass. The
sound of the radio filled the room like a pulse buried under the noise. It had
been there for months. Lately, it had grown clearer.

His neighbors came each Friday, bringing bread, envelopes, and quiet prayers.
They didn't say why. They didn't need to. Everyone felt something. A movement
in the air. A shift beneath the ordinary. Tonight, an older woman pressed a
folded envelope into his hand without a word. Her palm was cool. Her eyes held
a knowing that didn't require language.

Noah placed the envelope beside his prayer book and turned back to the static.
It deepened as he listened. Aware of him. He whispered the Shema. The
familiar words steadied his hands, but a truth settled in behind them like a
shadow. He would not be here much longer.

He turned the radio off for the first time in weeks. The room felt thinner
without it. The air sharper. He folded the envelope into his coat pocket and
stepped out into the snow. The sound of the static lingered in his ears like a
memory that refused to fade. There was a place he needed to be. He had already
begun to walk toward it.

Far away, under a different sky, another man was listening too. His static came
from the television, not the radio, but it was the same sound. Soft, alive, and
waiting.

The television picture had burned out long ago, but the speakers still hissed
through the night. Outside, the woods wrapped the small trailer in silence. His
boots sat by the door. The rifle leaned against the chair. Everything else in
the room had been stripped down over the years. Preparing for something he
couldn't name.

His son had died two winters ago. Cancer. A slow death that worked its way
inward. Patient. Thorough. He'd sat at that hospital bed and watched his boy
shrink into the sheets. Skin pale. Voice fading to whispers. He prayed through
every hour. Sometimes out loud. Sometimes with fists clenched so tightly his
palms split. He never stopped believing. Not once.

His wife had walked out before the treatments ended. She couldn't watch. He
still remembered the hallway where she stood. Hands trembling. Eyes lowered.
Her back finally turning away.

But God stayed.

The night his boy died, he came home to the television. The static was already
there, waiting. At first it was just noise. But over the months, it became
something else. A call. Not a voice he could name. But a presence that settled
in his chest the way scripture once did. He began to listen like Abraham once
listened. Without understanding, but with faith.

Tonight, the static broke for just a moment. In that stillness, he felt it
again. The weight of command. It wasn't a request. It was a call.

He rose, put on his blue baseball cap, and began to pack. A duffel. Some food.
Water. A Bible. The rifle. When the sun rose, he would leave. God called. He
would answer.
