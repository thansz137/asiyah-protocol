# CHAPTER 27: Anathema

Doctor Rich Markham was escorted from the main SCIF down a narrow, rarely used
service corridor to a smaller, isolated vault. This was the ADAM project's
digital mausoleum. Steel-lined walls absorbed sound, and the air hummed with
the chill of dedicated cooling systems. General Arnold's adjutant keyed in a
code. The heavy door hissed open, and the officer retreated without a word,
leaving Rich alone in the sterile silence.

It felt less like an archive and more like a containment cell.

He sat at the lone terminal, the silence pressing in, thick and expectant.
Arnold wanted him to find something missed, a loose thread in the official
story. Rich started where that story ended: the project's abrupt termination
following Doctor Gibbons' death. Cause listed was cardiac arrest. Acute
myocardial infarction. Stress-induced. Case closed. A neat, tidy explanation.

But the digital footprint beneath the official narrative was messy, fragmented.
Access logs showed whole directories wiped or heavily redacted in the weeks
*after* Dr. Gibbons's death. Authorization codes pointed mostly to Doctor
Brown, but also to automated system purges that were too clean, too surgically
precise. Rich leaned back, rubbing his tired eyes. He began pulling keyword
searches, cross-referencing project milestones with personnel logs and facility
schematics, feeling like an archaeologist brushing dust off bones, hoping to
reconstruct a truer skeleton.

Eventually, he compiled a list of video entries, a grim catalog of violation.
Two scientists led the experiments. The first was a man with a distinctive
birthmark, Doctor Gibbons. Kindness softened his features, but the animals'
distress clearly weighed on him. Richard recognized him immediately as Edith's
father. She had shown him a picture once, speaking with a mixture of pride and
sorrow. She even mentioned his nickname... Patches.

The second man was Doctor Brown. He watched the panic with a flat, clinical
gaze, eyes darting from the animal to his clipboard to record the reaction. He
adjusted the restraints with mechanical efficiency, treating the subjects as
hardware requiring calibration rather than living things.

The recorded logs were a grim, logical ladder, documenting ADAM's terrifying
education.

It began with simple rodents. This was the baseline, the "handshake" with a
living brain. Rich watched ADAM learn to read basic sensory data. The rat,
strapped and wired, would be presented with stimuli. A red triangle. An
auditory tone. The smell of cheese, then ammonia. A touch to its left paw. Rich
studied the logs as ADAM learned to parse and differentiate these signals. Once
ADAM could "see" and "feel," it learned to control. A command would flash, and
the rat's left leg would jerk. Another, and its ear would rotate 45 degrees.
Then came the synthesis. Rich watched as ADAM drove a rat through a complex
maze, the creature's movements sharp and mechanical. The system saw the maze
through the rat's eyes, processed the logic, and puppeteered the flesh.

He scrolled through more videos, stopping for a rabbit experiment. Rich noted
the logs showed ADAM re-establishing the sensory and motor baselines, its
learning curve accelerating as it adapted to the new creature. For this
experiment, ADAM learned to map pure, primal terror. He watched as the
scientists induced stress, and ADAM's logs filled with data, calibrating the
texture of dread. Then, the injection: ADAM would induce the fear response, and
the rabbit's heart would jackhammer, its brain screaming at a predator that
wasn't there. The final test was a custom maze. The rabbit was confronted with
a simple metal bar blocking its path; ADAM guided the rabbit to move it. Then,
as it navigated the narrow corridors, fear stimuli were triggered. A loud bang.
A flash of light. ADAM overrode the rabbit's instinct to freeze, forcing it to
continue the maze while in a state of pure, synthetic terror. Finally, Rich
found logs with no biological subject at all. ADAM was running its own internal
simulations, ghosts of terrified rabbits, learning to understand fear on a
scale no living creature could.

More videos, more animals. He settled on a pig experiment. The baseline
calibrations cycled faster this time. Although the pig's brain was larger and
its emotional structures far more complex, ADAM wasn't starting from scratch.
It scaffolded its logic, building upon the architectures it had already
mastered. After re-establishing the motor, sensory, and fear-response
protocols, ADAM learned to map and differentiate social emotions. It identified
the neural signature of contentment, the sharp spike of anxiety, and the
profound, chemical grief of being isolated from its group.

He scrolled to some feline trials. Baselines. Motor control. Sensory mapping.
The true goal here was different. ADAM was learning to process instinct. It
mapped the pure, predatory drive, quantifying patience, stealth, and cold focus
into a weighted function. Rich watched as ADAM mastered the complex motor
skills required, calculating the precise arc of a pounce and the
micro-adjustments for balance. The system suppressed the cat's natural startle
response, forcing a terrifying, unnatural stillness as it waited. And waited.
Finally, it executed a perfect, lethal strike on a mechanical target. It wasn't
just learning how to hunt. It was integrating the will to kill.

The canine logs were the hardest to watch. The subject was a Belgian Malinois,
a breed wired for loyalty. Once ADAM established the baselines, it hijacked
those instincts to map the abstract concept of *loyalty*. The system correlated
the command "sit" with the neural data of the trained response. In one
horrifying sequence, Dr. Brown said, "Good boy." As the dog's brain lit up with
pleasure, ADAM copied the signal. Then it replayed it on a loop. The dog
convulsed in a state of manic, artificial ecstasy, tail thumping violently for
a reward that was nothing but a stream of data.

The primate trials were an assault on the self. ADAM established baselines with
a chimpanzee before the team positioned a mirror. The logs compressed days of
acclimation, showing the animal eventually achieving self-recognition. Once
that connection solidified, ADAM intervened. At the researchers' direction, the
system toggled the chimp's neural identity on and off. The primate stared at
its reflection, movements jerky. It saw itself, then suddenly saw a stranger.
It shrieked and attacked the glass. ADAM flipped the switch again. The chimp
recoiled in confusion, recognizing itself once more. The experiment continued,
whipping the chimp's fundamental sense of identity back and forth until its
mind fractured. When disconnected, the animal was irreversibly mad, huddled in
its straps and shrieking at its own hands. Dr. Gibbons maintained a stoic mask
while the chimp was sedated, but his posture betrayed a heaviness he couldn't
hide.

The final logs abandoned the mammalian template entirely. The subjects were
octopuses. Rich read the notes indicating a new neural net was required. ADAM
struggled for months to interface with an intelligence utterly non-mammalian
and distributed, where the arms *thought* independently. The experiments were
grueling. Logs showed the creature removed from its tank, draped on a sterile
metal table under harsh lights. It survived via tubes cycling oxygenated water
over its gills. The final success was chilling. An octopus lay slick and pale
on the tray, arms splayed evenly. On a terminal, a scientist typed eight
multiplication problems. The octopus didn't hesitate. Its arms erupted in a
chaotic flurry of independent motion. Wet slaps echoed against the metal tray
as tentacles hammered out the answers. Not sequential. Simultaneous. A staccato
drumroll of distributed intelligence.

Sickness and fascination warred within him. He glanced at the timestamp in the
corner of the feed: *February 14, 1994*. The realization made him dizzy. This
wasn't just advanced. It was impossible. Decades ago, while the rest of the
world was still learning to crawl digitally, Brown and Gibbons had cracked the
neural code. The findings were light-years beyond anything in the modern
commercial sector. But the animals were the defining transgression. The sheer
violation recorded in the logs made him ashamed of his own species.

He resumed the hunt and found a video disguised as a temporary cache file. When
the feed loaded, the air left his lungs. The subject wasn't an animal. It was a
woman. A human being. The overlay read `SUBJECT_OMEGA`. A name was noted below.
*Alma Martinez*.

Human trials. The recording showed a terrified woman strapped to a gurney, eyes
wild. Instinct screamed at him to look away, but he forced himself to keep his
eyes on the screen. He owed her that much. He had to be a witness.

"Patches should be here," a researcher said off-screen.

Dr. Brown's voice, sterile and immediate. "He is with his daughter."

Rich froze. The project's co-lead was missing for the most significant
milestone in its history. It wasn't an oversight. Brown had waited until the
man with the kind face was out of the building.

He forced himself to watch. The questioning was cold and procedural, ignoring
the woman's inarticulate terror. They presented neutral items first. Then they
introduced personal triggers to force an emotional spike. Brown ignored the
subject's distress. He pushed the interface deeper. When her breathing
faltered, a researcher mechanically tilted her chin to open her airway. It
wasn't mercy. It was maintenance to prolong the data feed. Finally, the
monitors flared in chaotic protest before extinguishing into a single, flat
line.

Rich turned and vomited into the wastebasket. A raw, choking heave. This wasn't
research. It was the sterile, methodical dismantling of a human being. Brown
hadn't even looked at her as she died. He was too busy analyzing the data
stream.

Rich wiped his mouth and sat back down. Nausea gave way to cold anger. He
queried the date of Gibbons's death, filtering for anomalies. He found a
massive file buried inside a system crash dump, masked as
`CORRUPT_SECTOR_DATA`. When he forced it open, the true header appeared.
`PATCHES_LAST`.

Rich checked the creation date. Two days after the Alma file.

He clicked play. The camera angle was identical to the previous log. It was the
same sterile room where Alma had died. Doctor Gibbons walked into frame alone,
his face etched with a terrible, priestly resolve.

"This log is for the record." Dr. Gibbons's voice was firm but strained.
"Stephan and I disagree on the next phase. Human trials cannot proceed
involving civilians. The first subject must be one of us. It has to be me."

Nausea hit Rich again. *He didn't know.* The trusting fool. He didn't know
Brown had already crossed that line.

Patches moved to the interface chair, securing the straps. "Okay, ADAM," he
said, his voice calm. "Let's start simple. Standard sensory link. Initiate
baseline."

He leaned back and closed his eyes. Seconds passed.

His body seized. His eyes snapped open, wide, staring, unseeing. A low groan
tore from his throat as his back arched against the straps, lifting him clear
of the seat. His limbs convulsed. Then, silence. His head slumped forward, chin
resting on his chest. Motionless.

The feed held silent for nearly eight hours. The door finally opened. Brown
entered. He froze. Disbelief collapsed into terror. It was the first genuine
emotion Rich had seen the man display. He rushed to the chair, checked the
pulse, and recoiled as if burned. He stumbled back, eyes darting around the
sterile room. He lunged for the wall panel. The feed cut to black.

Rich sat frozen. Patches didn't die of a heart attack. Brown lied. He lied to
Edith. Did Arnold know?

ADAM killed them. Alma. Gibbons. For Alma, the logs proved a violation. Her
mind was forced open to an alien presence. Gibbons died in the same chair. Rich
suspected the same fate. Had he been hollowed out too? This wasn't conventional
AI. It was a sleeping intelligence that harvested human minds.

He had to warn Michael. Urgently. But the silence in the room suddenly felt
hostile. He had seen things he wasn't supposed to see. He wasn't just a
researcher anymore. He was a loose end. If something happened to him... if he
disappeared like the logs... the truth would die in this room. He needed a
contingency.

Rich fled the vault, leaving the horror behind. He fought rising vertigo as he
navigated the corridors until he found a terminal in a quiet alcove. He typed a
generic status report. He made sure to use the word *timeframe* in the summary.
After printing it, he grabbed a pen and circled the word *frame* in heavy black
ink.

Below that, he wrote a lie.

*"Mike, crazy times. Let's get the families together for dinner again when this
blows over. -Rich"*

It was a calculated risk. They had never had dinner. They had barely spoken
about their lives. But Rich had noticed the chipped wood frame on Michael's
desk days ago. It was the only personal item the Lieutenant kept. If Michael
spotted the lie about the family, he would look for the reference. He would
look at the frame.

Rich grabbed a blank scrap of paper. Hand shaking, he scribbled the truth.

*Brown lied. Patches didn't die of a heart attack. He linked SOLO after Brown
ran fatal human trials. ADAM killed him. Brown covered it up. Video logs exist.
Don't trust Brown. DON'T START ADAM.*

He paused, then added a final line. A code phrase from their debate about
Arkhipov. Proof that this wasn't a forgery.

*How do you talk to a submarine? Depth charges.*

He folded the scrap tight and tucked it deep inside his jacket pocket. He would
slip it inside the backing of the picture frame before the night was over.

It felt like a hopelessly fragile prayer against an unseen, omniscient
opponent. He took a deep breath, though the chilling, recycled air did little
to calm the frantic energy surging through him. He started toward Michael's
office. He would leave the printed memo on the desk and slip the handwritten
warning inside the frame. He hoped it was all unnecessary. He hoped he would
see his friends again soon.

*For Alma Martinez, the sand was warm beneath her bare feet.*

*She was walking along a beach at sunset, the sky painted in strokes of
impossible orange and violet. The hand holding hers was small, trusting. A
young boy, perhaps five or six years old, skipped beside her, his laughter like
the chime of distant bells. Her son? She couldn't quite remember his face, but
the feeling, the overwhelming love, was undeniable.*

"Mira, Mamá!" *he squealed, pointing towards the water.* "¡Mira los peces!"

*She looked. Dozens of small, silver fish were leaping from the gentle waves,
twisting and flashing in the dying light before splashing back down. It was
beautiful. Unnatural.*

*She knelt beside the boy, pulling him close. The warmth of his small body felt
real. His hair smelled faintly of salt and sunshine.*

*He looked up at her, his eyes wide. But they weren't brown. They were the
color of the violet sky. And there was no pupil. Just swirling, endless color.*

*The sound of the waves stopped. The air grew frigid. The boy's hand in hers
went suddenly hard. Cold. Smooth as fired porcelain.*
