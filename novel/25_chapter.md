# CHAPTER 25: Diaspora

The silence in the new briefing room was worse than the chaos.

They had been moved from the UN observation suite to a windowless SCIF two
levels beneath the White House. The room smelled of industrial carpet and
stale, recycled air. For seventy-two hours, they had done nothing but watch the
world come apart.

Michael stared at the main monitor. It showed a mosaic of global feeds. Chinese
troops stacking concrete barriers at the Mongolian border. The Russian
Federation declaring a "temporary communications blackout" for all civilian
networks. French fighters scrambling over the English Channel in response to an
unresponsive cargo plane.

The world wasn't screaming. It was holding its breath.

"The cyber attacks are the worst part," Jennifer said, rubbing her eyes. Her
voice was raw. "It's not one group. It's everyone. Financial markets are in
freefall. Water treatment plants in Germany, power grids in Ohio. Someone is
testing the fences."

"It's not 'someone,'" Edith Gibbons said, not looking up from her terminal. She
typed with a dry, rhythmic tapping. "It's a logical consequence of the
ultimatum. The 'Friends' removed the keystone. Now the arch is collapsing."

"They're *people*, Edith," Jennifer snapped. "They're terrified. They're not a
'logical consequence.' They're stocking up on food. They're trying to find
their families. The panic is the problem."

"The panic is a symptom," Edith countered, her voice flat. "The *problem* is
that we are a Type 0 civilization trying to negotiate with what is, at minimum,
a Type II. We are ants bargaining with a boot. The ultimatum, 'change or be
removed,' wasn't a threat. It was a diagnosis."

Rich Markham, who had been pale and silent since New York, finally spoke. "A
diagnosis? They murdered eleven people on a live feed, Edith. They threatened
to exterminate us."

"Exactly," Edith said, spinning in her chair to face them. "It's a problem of
resource and scale. We cannot process what's happening fast enough. We are
sifting through *exabytes* of new data: signal chatter, atmospheric anomalies,
biological reports on the envoys. And we are using cocktail napkins. We are
outmatched."

Michael saw where this was going. He looked at Jennifer, whose jaw was tight.

"We need a better negotiating tool," Edith continued. "We need a faster brain.
We need something that can analyze their threat and model a response."

"Analyze *what*?" Jennifer shot back. "We have nothing. We're blind. All we
have is a threat and eleven corpses."

"No," Edith said, her gaze hardening. "We have one thing. We have ADAM."

The name hung in the air, cold and heavy.

Before Michael could respond, the door to the SCIF hissed open. General Arnold
entered, and he looked like a man who hadn't slept since the 'Friends' had been
named. His uniform was rumpled, his eyes bloodshot.

"Report," he barked.

"The world is fracturing, sir," Michael said, standing. "Global military and
civilian infrastructure is degrading. We believe the cyber attacks are
opportunistic, not coordinated by the... by the 'Friends.'"

"They're walling themselves off," Arnold growled, gesturing to the screen.
"Every nation for itself. The President is trying to hold the G7 together, but
it's a fool's errand. What do we have?"

Michael paused. He looked at Edith.

"Dr. Gibbons has a proposal, sir," Michael said.

Edith didn't wait. "General, we're blind. We're losing the intelligence war
because we're not processing the data. We need to reactivate ADAM."

The change in Arnold was immediate and violent. The exhaustion evaporated,
replaced by a cold fury that silenced the room.

"Absolutely not," he said, his voice quiet, which was worse than a shout.

"Sir, it is our only high-level analysis tool that isn't human-bound," Edith
pressed, standing to meet his glare. "It's our only chance."

"Dr. Gibbons," Arnold said, taking a step toward her. "The ADAM project was a
catastrophic failure. It was a money pit that produced nothing but noise, and
it *cost* this country one of its greatest assets. It cost you your father."

Edith flinched, her composure cracking for the first time. "My father's death
was a tragedy, sir. It was a heart attack..."

"Brought on by obsession!" Arnold slammed his hand on the table. The monitors
rattled. "He was chasing a ghost in a box, and it killed him. That program is a
graveyard, Doctor. I will not send more good people to die in it. The subject
is closed."

"Extinction is also absolute, General!" Edith shot back, her voice rising. "We
are facing *extinction*. ADAM is our only..."

"It's *not* our only option," Jennifer interjected, stepping between them. Her
voice was firm, siding with the General. "It's a risk we can't afford. If that
thing is half as unstable as the reports say, turning it on in the middle of
this crisis could be throwing gasoline on a fire. We don't even know if the
'Friends' are *still watching*."

Edith turned to Jennifer. She stared at the analyst, her lip curling in a
faint, dismissive sneer. "You're thinking about 'risk.' You're still thinking
on a human scale. This is a *species-level* threat. We need a species-level
defense."

Neither woman moved. Edith squared her shoulders, chin lifted in a sharp
challenge, while Jennifer held the scientist's gaze with icy patience. Michael
looked between them. The schism wasn't just on the screens anymore. It was
sitting at their table.

"Sir," Michael said, breaking the silence. Arnold turned his glare on him. "Dr.
Gibbons has a point. And so does Ms. Smith. But we're paralyzed. We're not
making a decision, we're just... waiting."

The exhaustion of the last seventy-two hours throbbed behind his eyes. The
image of Lazarus shambling out of the dark ghosted through his mind, pressing
down on him with the weight of the inevitable.

"What if we don't 'use' it?" Michael proposed. "What if we just see if it's
viable? Dr. Brown is still alive. He's the only one who could get it online. We
send a team. Just to assess. If it's a dead end, we walk away. If it's not,
we'll have an option. We need an option, General. Right now, we have none."

General Arnold held the silence. He watched the map, tracking the flickering
icons of border closures and grid failures like a commander watching his lines
break. He shifted his glare to Edith, his jaw grinding, before returning to
Michael.

"Fine." The word sounded like a curse. "I'm authorizing a field operation. Your
entire team. You'll find Dr. Brown and assess the ADAM program."

Michael nodded. "Sir, I'd like to propose an alteration. Dr. Markham's value
is in analysis. Sending him into the field with us is a waste of resources.
He'd be more effective here, providing a fresh set of eyes on the data we have,
supporting us from the SCIF. He's not prejudiced by the project's history like
Dr. Gibbons."

The General considered it for a moment, his gaze shifting to Markham. The older
man's shoulders dropped an inch. "That's a sound tactical decision, Lieutenant.
Fine. Markham, you stay. You'll have full access to our internal database on
the project. Find me something we missed."

He turned to Edith as he walked to the door. "You will find Dr. Brown. You will
assess the ADAM program. You will *not* interface with it, you will *not*
attempt to reactivate it without my direct authorization. If you violate that
order, I will personally have you arrested for treason. Is that clear, Doctor?"

The General didn't wait for an answer. "Markham, you report to my adjutant. The
rest of you, get your gear."

The door hissed shut, leaving the three of them in the silence. Jennifer shoved
her tablet into her case, keeping her back to the scientist. Edith didn't
notice. She was already at the terminal, typing.

Michael stared at the closed door. The name *Lazarus* turned over in his mind.
He didn't feel like a soldier preparing for an operation. He felt like a man
who had just volunteered for a suicide mission.

*The tile was cold. Not a familiar, morning cold, but a flat, penetrating cold
that did not seem to come* **from** *the floor so much as it was the*
**property** *of the floor itself.*

*Alma smelled chocolate.*

*It was the rich, slightly burnt scent of the abuelita mix she made for her
daughters on feast days. She heard them then, not as a memory but as a
presence, their laughter like the sound of small, silver spoons clinking
against glass, just on the other side of the kitchen pantry.*

*"*Mijas?*" she whispered. The sound was thin and processed, like a
low-fidelity recording played in an empty room.*

*She reached for the pantry door, her fingers brushing the cheap, flaking wood.*

*The doorknob was not there.*

*There was only a smooth, uninterrupted plane of wood. She knocked, then
pounded, the sound a dull, thick* **thud** *that seemed to be instantly
swallowed by the air.*

*"*Niñas, abran!*"*

*The laughter tinkled again, but this time it was different. It was coming
from* **inside** *the refrigerator. She turned, a sudden, sick vertigo washing
over her. The kitchen lights spasmed. In the stuttering glare, the ceiling
dissolved. The walls shot upward, disappearing into a vertical abyss that
pulsed with the sound of a million flies trapped behind glass. The smell of
chocolate vanished, replaced by the sharp, metallic tang of scorched copper.*
