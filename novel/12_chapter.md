# CHAPTER 12: Entanglement

The hallway outside was a blur of shouting voices and boots hitting tile.
Inside, the breakout room felt like a coffin.

Michael didn't sit. He paced the length of the mahogany table, adrenaline
chewing through his fatigue. The silence in the room was heavy, pressurized by
the chaos just outside the door.

"We are bleeding time," Michael said, stopping to place both hands on the
table. He looked at each of them in turn. "Our mandate is defense. If the Envoy
hit the First Daughter, we need to know how. And we need to know if he can do
it again."

"We cannot view this as a conflict," Dr. Markham said. He was staring at a knot
in the mahogany table, refusing to look up. "We need to wipe that idea from our
minds."

Michael sighed, frustration tightening his chest. "We have an obligation to
fight, Doctor. We protect the population. That is the oath. We cannot just give
up."

"Please. Call me Rich." Markham finally looked up, locking eyes with Michael.
"And I am not talking about giving up. I am talking about reality. Humor me.
One thought experiment. Pit the US military of 1918 against the US military of
today. Who wins?"

Michael frowned, annoyed by the diversion. "We do. Obviously."

"Correct. But look at *how*." Markham leaned forward, his voice dropping to a
hushed intensity. "General Black Jack Pershing rides onto the field. He has
bolt-action rifles and horses. We meet him with a flat-screen monitor standing
in the mud. The screen turns on. It is General Arnold, broadcasting from a
secure bunker in high definition."

Markham's hands moved, sketching the scene in the air. "Pershing doesn't see
technology. He has no concept of pixels or bandwidth. He sees a magic mirror.
He sees an oracle. Arnold tells him: *I can see your horses. I can see your
artillery. I am watching you from the stars.* Pershing looks up, but he doesn't
see satellites. He sees the heavens."

"Then Arnold presses a button," Markham continued, his cadence speeding up. "A
cruise missile, launched from a thousand miles away, screams in at Mach 4.
Pershing never hears it. He never sees it. One second he is standing there,
confused by the magic mirror. The next second? Pink mist. Complete
vaporization."

Markham slammed his hand on the table. The sound made Edith jump.

"That is a one-century gap, Michael. One hundred years turns warfare into
magic. We wouldn't suffer a single casualty. It wouldn't be a war. It would be
an extermination."

Markham gestured vaguely at the ceiling, toward the sky above the White House.

"Now imagine a civilization one million years ahead of us. We aren't even
General Pershing. We are the ants on the lawn. If we try to fight them, we
won't even know what killed us."

"Point taken," Michael said, his jaw tight. "But that doesn't change the
mission. Are you suggesting we just open the doors and let the Envoy do
whatever he wants?"

"No. But we can meet them halfway," Rich replied, his voice shaking slightly as
the adrenaline of his speech wore off. "Todd's request to see his family wasn't
a threat. It was a human request. Did we really have to deny him?"

"That wasn't our call," Michael said. "But we need to move forward. If access
to the family is the lever, maybe we pull it."

Jennifer spoke up, her eyes glued to her screen. "I'd be careful with that
lever, Mike. We don't know what condition the family is in. If we show them to
Todd and they've been mistreated? That might just pour gasoline on the fire."

The only sound in the room was the low, electric hum of the server stacks.
Michael looked around, waiting for someone to deny the possibility. Rich stared
at the table, refusing to meet his eyes, while Jennifer kept her gaze locked on
the scrolling data stream.

"Find them," Michael ordered, pointing to her terminal. "Use the overriding
clearance. If they are hurt, I need to know now."

"Already on it," Jennifer said, typing furiously.

"Stop guessing," Edith cut in. She didn't look at Michael, her eyes fixed on
the ceiling as if reading invisible notes. "You're thinking like soldiers. You
need to think like biologists."

"Edith..." Michael warned.

"Todd asked for his family," she barreled on, ignoring him. "Why? If his
masters are gods who can see us from orbit... if they can reach down to the
South Lawn and strike a girl unconscious... why do they need to ask where his
wife is?"

Michael stopped pacing. He looked at Edith, then back to Rich.

Rich adjusted his glasses, catching up to her logic. "Because he *didn't
know*."

"Exactly," Edith said. A small, predatory smile touched her lips. "Blind spots.
They have limitations. They can see the troops on the field, maybe. But they
can't see everything."

"I have them," Jennifer announced. The clatter of keys stopped. "FBI swept them
up hours ago. They're at a black site in the Midwest. Medical intake says they
are shaken and confused, but physically unharmed. Standard isolation protocols.
They're in the dark."

"It doesn't matter why," Michael said, cutting off the debate. He turned to the
wall monitor, his mind racing. "Edith is right. If they have blind spots, they
aren't omniscient gods. They are observing us. That implies a physical process.
And if there is a process, there is a footprint."

He turned to Jennifer. "Test the theory. Bring up the signal data from the Oval
Office. The one hundred thirty-seven count. Then overlay the medical telemetry
for the President's daughter. Do we even have access to that?"

"We do," Jennifer said. She didn't look up, fingers flying across the keyboard.
"With FPCON Delta active, Walter Reed is piping all vital telemetry into the
secure command network automatically. I'm pulling the neural feed now."

Jennifer tapped a key. Two topographic maps appeared on the wall monitor. On
the left, the chaotic red static of Todd's neural scan. On the right, the
frantic, thready rhythm of a young girl's brain waves.

"Look at the timestamps," Michael said, pointing to the screen.

A massive red spike erupted from Todd's scan. *One hundred thirty-seven.*

In the exact same microsecond, the girl's scan snapped into the violent rhythm.
Her brain waves spiked in perfect unison with his. It wasn't just a reaction.
It was a mirror.

"It is synchronization," Edith whispered. The color had drained from her face.
"The signal manipulating his mind is broadcasting to hers." She stepped closer
to the screen. "But look at the amplitude. The pattern is identical, but the
result is different. Todd is walking and talking. The girl is comatose."

"He is the native hardware," Edith said, her voice trembling. "He surfs the
wave. She drowns in it."

"I agree," Rich added, cleaning his glasses nervously. "But I suspect it is not
just physiology. These could be two distinct signals sharing the same carrier
rhythm. The pulse is identical... the one-thirty-seven count... but the encoded
instructions between the spikes could be completely different. One signal
commands Todd. The other attacks the girl. We just lack the resolution to
distinguish them."

"Repeater or receiver, it doesn't matter," Michael said. The implications
chilled him. "Whether Todd is the source or the target, the lightning strikes
where he is standing. If he stays in the White House, the building isn't
secure."

He turned back to the table. The hesitation was gone.

"I'm going to make a recommendation to General Arnold," Michael announced. "We
need to move Todd's family from the Midwest to a secure facility outside the
Beltway. Then we move Todd to meet them there. We tell him it's to see his
family. It's the truth, and it gets that signal away from the leadership."

He looked at the team. Jennifer nodded immediately. Edith stared at the screen,
terrified and fascinated, before giving a sharp nod. Rich looked pale, but he
squared his shoulders and agreed.

"Good," Michael said. He reached for the secure phone at the center of the
table. "I'm calling the general."
