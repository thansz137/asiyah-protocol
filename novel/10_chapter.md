# CHAPTER 10: The Pulse

The Tactical Breakout Room was quieter than a church and twice as cold. Michael
Grant sat alone at the long mahogany table. The only light came from the
standby LEDs on the secure terminals.

He stared at his reflection in the black glass of the monitor. The adrenaline
that had sustained him since the morning had soured into a heavy, toxic
fatigue. His hands felt light. His head felt heavy. He was vibrating with the
specific, nauseating exhaustion that comes after a brush with history.

The magnetic lock on the door disengaged with a heavy steel *thud*.

Michael scrambled to his feet as Dr. Edith Gibbons stepped inside. Her escort
remained in the hall. The heavy door sealed them back in the silence.

"Dr. Gibbons." Michael tried to summon his usual charm. He tried to smile. The
muscles in his face felt numb. He suspected he looked more like a man suffering
a mild stroke than a dashing intelligence officer.

The biologist ignored the attempt. She sat down, placing a notepad on the table
with deliberate precision. "Why are you here, Lieutenant? You are low-level
intel. A dime a dozen. What gives?"

Michael appreciated the lack of polite friction. "I can't go into details. But
I am as surprised as you are." He sat back down, wincing as a vertebra popped
in his lower back. "And you are here for DARPA?"

"I presume so."

"Not a big believer in God either?" he risked.

Edith paused. She looked at him, really looked at him, for the first time. "I
do believe in God. I am Catholic."

Michael blinked. "Catholic? After the way you dismantled that Pastor?"

"Yes. Why does that surprise you?"

"You called a famous preacher an idiot for believing in a deity he cannot see."

"No. I pointed out his hypocrisy." She adjusted her glasses. "He measures this
alien contact with a ruler made of skepticism. Yet he measures his own faith
with a ruler made of hope. We would not be in this bunker unless there was hard
evidence of contact. It may not fit our comfortable worldview. But reality
rarely does."

"Point taken. Still. You didn't make any friends."

"Does it look like I have a lot of friends?"

Her voice was dry, but the corner of her mouth twitched upward. It was the
ghost of a smile.

The lock thudded again. The air in the room seemed to thicken. General Arnold
entered, trailing Jennifer Smith and Dr. Richard Markham like a storm front.

Michael stood at attention.

"Sit," the General barked. He did not look at them. He stared at the wood grain
of the table as if it had personally offended him.

Michael sat. He caught Jennifer's eye. She looked pale. Not tired. *Haunted*.

"We are breaking into tactical teams," the General said to the table. "Ms.
Smith was the initial contact. Lieutenant Grant accompanied. We have captured
audio and biometric data from the encounter."

He gestured vaguely at Jennifer. "Give them the summary."

Jennifer didn't look up from her tablet. "During the transport from Illinois,
the subject was compliant. Heart rate remained between sixty and seventy beats
per minute. Respiration was normal. We observed no anomalies in the
bio-readings during the flight."

She stopped.

Edith leaned forward. She narrowed her eyes. "That's it? You had the most
important intelligence asset in human history in a containment vehicle for
three hours. And all you have for me is his resting heart rate?"

Jennifer hesitated. She glanced at the General, then back to her screen. "The
subject slept for most of the duration, Doctor. The data is consistent with a
normal human male."

"I don't buy it," Edith snapped. "You are the NSA. You don't just measure
pulses. You measure thoughts. I know what you have in that toy box of yours."

Jennifer shifted in her seat. She looked uncomfortable. "The passive monitoring
systems were... inactive during transport."

"But they weren't inactive upstairs, were they?" Edith pressed. She sensed
blood in the water. "We just spent an hour in the Situation Room with the
subject. Are you telling me you didn't have the sensors running *there*?"

Jennifer looked at the General. She was asking for permission.

The General sighed. He rubbed his temples. "Show them, Smith. If we are going
to use the eggheads, we might as well give them the eggs."

Jennifer typed a command. "This data was recorded forty minutes ago," she said
softly. "During the meeting with the President."

The wall monitor flared to life.

It was chaos.

The screen displayed a topographic map of a brain, but it was unrecognizable.
It looked like a storm of static. A violent, thrashing mess of red and white
noise that washed out the entire image.

"Equipment failure?" Michael asked.

"No," Edith whispered. She stood up and walked to the screen. "Look at the
amplitude. That's not noise. That is a grand mal seizure. He should be on the
floor foaming at the mouth."

"He wasn't seizing," Michael said. "He was sitting in a chair. He was talking."

"Not with that brain he wasn't," Edith said, pointing to the red static. "That
is a chaotic event. There is no structure. It is just a biological fire."

"Wait," Richard Markham said. He took off his glasses and began to polish them
on his shirt. His hands were shaking. "Jennifer. The refresh rate on the
monitor. It's too slow. That isn't static."

"What is it?" Jennifer asked.

"It's speed," Richard said. "The event is happening too fast for the render.
Slow it down. Expand the timeline by a factor of a thousand."

Jennifer's fingers blurred across the glass. She began building the query
string, layering filters over the raw feed.

"Good Lord," Edith muttered. She stood up, her shadow falling over the console.
"We don't have time for hunting and pecking. Move aside."

She reached for the terminal.

Jennifer didn't look up. Her left hand shot out and intercepted Edith's wrist
inches from the glass. It wasn't a gentle block. It was a kinetic stop.

"Sit down," Jennifer said. She didn't raise her voice. "You don't know the
syntax. You will only corrupt the cache."

Edith stared at her wrist, then at the young woman holding it. She pulled her
arm back, nursing the joint, and slowly lowered herself into her chair.

Jennifer finished the sequence and hit execute.

The static vanished.

The chaotic white noise stretched out, revealing deep, dark valleys of silence
interrupted by violent, impossible spikes.

"Play the audio," Edith commanded.

The speakers crackled. Todd's voice filled the room. It was calm. Flat.
Generic.

On the screen, the brain screamed.

A massive, crimson spike erupted from the blackness of the dormant mind. It
slammed upward, held for a microsecond, and crashed back down.

*Thump.*

Silence. The line went flat.

*Thump.*

"It's rhythmic," Edith whispered.

"It's counting," Richard said. He stood up, drawn to the light like a moth.
"Jennifer. Put a counter on the peaks."

Numbers flashed over the violent red spikes.

*Two.*

*Three.*

The room was silent. The only sound was the wet, heavy thud of the signal
hitting the screen.

*Five.*

*Seven.*

"They are primes," Richard said. He looked sick. "It is counting prime
numbers."

*Eleven.*

*Thirteen.*

"How high does it go?" Michael asked. He felt a phantom heat on his skin. He
had been in that room. He had been swimming in that signal. But it wasn't just
the alien broadcast that rattled him. It was the silence of his superiors.
Jennifer knew. The General knew. While Michael had sat there, thinking he was
part of the team, the walls had been stripping him bare. They had recorded
every spike of fear in his own mind, and he hadn't even felt the prick.

"Just watch," Richard said.

The numbers climbed. The rhythm was relentless. It was not biological. It was
the cold, hard ticking of a machine.

*Seventeen.*

*Nineteen.*

The count went on. It felt violating. It felt like watching something crawl
under the skin of the world.

*Twenty-three.*

*Twenty-nine.*

The General stared at the screen. His jaw was set so hard a muscle jumped in
his cheek. He didn't say a word.

The count accelerated. The red spikes merged into a wall of violence.

*Eighty-three... Eighty-nine... Ninety-seven...*

The screen flashed white. A massive, singular pulse that wiped the data clean.

The cycle reset.

*Two.*

"The sequence terminates at one hundred thirty-seven. The fine-structure
constant. It is the signature of the universe. That isn't a thought, General.
That is a broadcast signal hardcoded into his grey matter."

"Is it a loop?" Richard asked. He was sweating now. "Jennifer. Overlay the next
cycle. See if it evolves."

Jennifer swiped the screen, dragging the fresh block of data over the previous
one. The red spikes aligned. It was a perfect digital fit.

"Zero deviation," she said.

"Check the whole file," Richard said. "Every timestamp. See if the pattern ever
breaks."

Jennifer expanded the query. The progress bar raced across the top of the
monitor. Search complete. Anomalies: 0.

"It is identical," Jennifer said. "Start to finish. It never changes. It just
repeats."

"He is a receiver," Edith said. She turned to face the room. "He is a meat
radio. And someone is broadcasting a high-frequency signal right into the Oval
Office."

The General didn't speak. He just scrubbed a hand down his face. The gesture
was slow, heavy, and steady.

"A receiver needs a transmitter," Richard said. He turned to Jennifer. "Did the
room sensors pick up a carrier wave? Anything on the RF spectrum?"

Jennifer shook her head. "The logs are clean. No spikes. No radiation. The room
was electronically dead."

"It wasn't dead," Richard said. He looked at the massive red spikes. "We just
have the wrong ears."

"Can we decode it?" Michael asked. "If it's a signal, there is a message inside
the pulses. Right?"

"It is dense," Edith said. She stared at the looping numbers. "The information
density between those prime spikes is staggering. It looks like compressed
code. We would need months to unpack a single second of this data."

She turned to the General. Her eyes were hard. "We need processing power. Real
power."

"Use the Cray at Oak Ridge," the General said dismissively.

"Not enough," Edith said. "The Cray is a calculator. You don't use a calculator
to read a book. This data isn't just math. It is a language. It is the most
complex text ever written, and the author isn't human."

She took a breath.

"I want to bring ADAM online."

Michael frowned. "Who is Adam?"

Jennifer looked confused. She glanced at Richard, who just shrugged.

The General froze. He looked at Edith slowly. The fatigue on his face was
replaced by something darker. "What did you say?"

"You heard me," Edith said. "ADAM was built for this. My father spent his life
building it for exactly this scenario."

"Your father died of a stress-induced heart attack because of that machine,"
the General said. His voice was low. Dangerous. "He worked himself into an
early grave chasing ghosts."

"Then let it be worth something!" Edith pleaded. "He died building the only
tool that can understand what is happening in that room. If we don't use it, he
died for nothing."

"He died because the project was a failure," the General snapped. "Dr. Brown
made a mess of it. It is a graveyard, Doctor. A broken machine."

"It is not broken. It is sleeping," Edith pressed. "Dr. Brown knows the
protocols. If anyone can wake it up, it is him. If we don't... if we don't, we
are flying blind against a species that can rewrite our neurology from orbit.
Please."

"I said no."

The General stood up. He grabbed his phone. His hands shook so bad he almost
dropped it. "ADAM stays dark. We will find another way."

He looked at the screen one last time. He looked at the math marching through
the mind of the man who had sat in the President's chair.

"I need to leave," he said distractedly.

He exited the room without another word. He left the heavy steel door wide
open.
