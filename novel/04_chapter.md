# CHAPTER 4: On a Pale Green Horse

**September 26, 1983 Serpukhov-15 bunker, near Moscow**

In the cramped, amber glow of the bunker, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov
stared at the radar screen. A single pale green dot pulsed in the center of the
grid.

It was a speck of electronic light, barely brighter than the background static,
traversing the black glass in silence. To a technician, it was a coordinate.
But Stanislav's stomach turned over. He remembered the forbidden whispers of
his grandmother. She taught him that when the Fourth Horseman rode out in
Revelation, the text didn't call him white or black. The original Greek called
him *chloros*. Pale green.

He watched the blip slide forward. It was a nuclear angel of death, riding that
pale green dot through the stratosphere, coming to harvest a city of millions
who were currently tucking their children into bed, completely unaware that the
end of history had just appeared on a monitor in Serpukhov-15.

The Americans had been testing the borders for months, flying bombers to the
edge of Soviet airspace just to see who flinched. It was a game of chicken
played with nuclear fire. Tensions were already at a breaking point. Just weeks
ago, a Soviet interceptor had shot down a commercial Korean airliner after
mistaking it for a spy plane. Hundreds of civilians burned because of a single
wrong identification. The ghost of that wreckage still haunted the room. One
mistake. One bad call. That was all it took to turn the world against them.

But now, for the US to launch only one missile was irrational. In all the
drills and war games, the Americans launched an overwhelming swarm to wipe out
the Soviet arsenal in one fell swoop. What was the point of launching just one
missile, horrifying as the aftermath would be? Was this another test, another
prodding of the bear?

The green dot moved forward.

Stanislav brought his hands to his throbbing temples. This decision would echo
for eternity through a nuclear wasteland. He had only to pick up the phone,
press a button, and notify the launch commander. From there, the dominoes
would fall according to Soviet nuclear doctrine. Underground silos would
rumble open. Submarines would surface from the ice. Long-range bombers would
veer to new coordinates. He had only to do his job, the one job that really
mattered, before it was too late to do anything at all.

But something was wrong. The logic didn't hold. He couldn't believe the
Americans would launch a solitary strike. The computerized early detection
system was just upgraded several days prior. Hadn't there been problems while
going through the hourly checklists? Perhaps the system was malfunctioning.

The green dot moved forward.

It wasn't Stanislav's decision to make. The process relied on him not making
decisions; that was the computer's job. He was an extension of that process. An
extension meant to relay information up the chain of command, that was all.

He extended a shaking hand to pick up the receiver, then fell back in his
chair. His temples throbbed to the point that he felt his head would explode.
He paused for a breath, a moment of time he didn't have to spare, then
straightened his back. In that instant, he had come to a conclusion. The launch
detection was a malfunction. To call his superiors would start a nuclear war,
all because some wires were crossed or a programming bug had been introduced.
He would not tip the first domino towards oblivion.

He eased back into his chair, watching the green dot continue its slow march
across the screen. It was a gamble. He was wagering the lives of millions on a
single gut instinct.

He closed his eyes and offered a silent plea to a God he wasn't supposed to
believe in. *Lord, let me be right.*
