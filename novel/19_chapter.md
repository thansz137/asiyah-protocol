# CHAPTER 19: The Dart

The amphetamines sat in Michael's stomach like a swallowed battery. He felt a
chemical hum under his skin that made standing still an act of discipline. The
facility wasn't rundown. It was nondescript. A gray box just outside the
Beltway designed to be forgotten.

Inside the observation room, the air smelled of antiseptic and floor wax. Two
men in white coats stood behind a bank of monitors. They didn't offer
handshakes. They didn't ask him to fill out forms.

"Dr. Arronson," the older man said, his eyes never leaving the screen. He
tapped a glass panel overlooking the conference room. "And Dr. Kay. We are
recording everything. You have twenty minutes."

Michael stepped closer to the glass. His jaw ached from clenching it. "What are
the rules?"

"There is a bulb on the table," Arronson said. "If it turns red, you leave.
Immediately. Do not finish your sentence. Do not say goodbye. You simply exit."

"And if I don't?"

Arronson finally looked at him. He stared with unblinking focus, even as a bead
of sweat tracked through the gray stubble on his cheek. "Then we purge the
room, Lieutenant. Don't make us do that."

The assistant opened the door to a corridor lined with opaque plastic sheeting.
It smelled of static. Michael walked the length of it. The drugs amplified the
sound of his boots. Every step thudded against his eardrums.

He reached for the far handle, then stopped. Habit forced his knuckles against
the steel. *Knock, knock.* He didn't wait for an answer.

The conference room was blindingly bright. Todd Miles sat at the center of a
steel table bolted to the floor. Deep, bruised crescents hung beneath his eyes,
yet he sat with a rigid, wire-taut posture. He didn't blink. His skin was gray
and waxy, pulled tight over his cheekbones. A blue vein pulsed at his temple,
keeping time with a rhythm Michael couldn't hear.

"Lt. Grant," Todd said. His voice was dry paper. "We meet again."

"May I?" Michael asked.

Todd gestured to the empty metal chair opposite him. "Please."

Michael sat. The chair was cold against his back. The room was devoid of
mirrors or visible lenses, but he knew Arronson was watching. A sealed water
bottle sat in front of Todd. The plastic was covered in condensation, but the
seal remained unbroken.

"Are they treating you well?" Michael asked.

"I've met my family," Todd said. "That is enough. Call me Todd."

"Mike."

"Mike," Todd repeated. He stared at Michael, his eyes unblinking. "What do you
want to know?"

"We need a name. What do we call them?"

"They don't have a name." Todd stared at the unlit bulb. "They want to help us.
To save the planet. Call them our Friends."

"Friends." Michael tasted the word. It felt wrong in his mouth, but he nodded.
"Okay. Friends. You said they made contact because we are facing a danger of
our own making. Be specific."

"Concern," Todd corrected. "They are concerned with optimization. Our species
has reached a terminal velocity. We are approaching a line where total systemic
collapse is a mathematical certainty."

Michael watched the vein pulse in Todd's temple. "Have they been watching us
long?"

"Does it matter?"

"We've had nuclear weapons for eighty years," Michael said. "We almost used
them in '62. The Cuban Missile Crisis. Did your Friends see that?"

Todd's head cocked to the side. A mechanical, bird-like movement. "They
observed. They... adjusted."

The confirmation hit Michael like a physical weight. *Arkhipov.*

"So they interfered," Michael said. "They stopped us from killing ourselves
then. Why reveal themselves now? We aren't staring down a launch order today."

"Because the equation has changed," Todd said. He rubbed his face with both
hands, the friction making a dry rasping sound. "In 1962, the threat was
binary. Launch or don't launch. A coin flip. They could nudge the coin."

Todd dropped his hands. His eyes were wet. "Now? The threat isn't a single
decision. It is the system itself."

Todd looked at his hands. "Three billion in 1965. Seven billion today. All of
them hungry. All of them burning energy to prove they exist."

He looked up, his eyes suddenly clear. "It is thermodynamics, Mike. You call it
climate change. They call it system failure. The feedback loops have already
closed. The ice melts, the dark water absorbs heat, the ice melts faster. It is
a death spiral."

Todd leaned closer, the smell of stale sweat wafting off him. "You are looking
for a brake pedal. But the train left the tracks ten years ago. You cannot vote
your way out of physics."

Michael remained quiet. The hum of the amphetamines seemed to pitch higher in
the silence.

"Behavioral sink," Todd whispered. "Calhoun's rats. You squeeze a population
too tight, and the social contract dissolves. Mothers eat their young. Males
hunt for sport. It isn't cruelty. It is biology."

Todd pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. When he pulled them away,
the skin around his sockets remained white. "I see it when I blink. The cities
burning. The water rising. And you... you want to know their *name*."

"We need to understand who we are dealing with," Michael said.

"You are struck by a poisoned dart," Todd said. He rested his forehead in his
palm, his voice muffled and thick. "Your limbs start to go numb. The doctor
comes to save you. But you refuse him. You demand to know the shooter's name.
You want the manufacturer of the dart. You want to know which lab synthesized
the toxin. You die asking questions that do not matter."

Todd leaned across the table. The condensation on the water bottle ran down
like tears. "You are the man on the ground, Mike. The poison is in your blood.
And you are wasting time asking about the shooter."

Todd slumped back, his chest heaving as if he had run a mile. The energy left
him as quickly as it had arrived.

"One last thing," Michael said. The drugs were fading, leaving a hollow ache in
his chest. "You talk about optimization. About equations."

Todd didn't answer. He just watched, his breathing shallow and wet.

"If that is how they think," Michael asked, "do they distinguish between a
biological mind and a synthetic one? Are humans the only things on Earth they
are talking to?"

Todd froze. The exhaustion vanished. His pupils dilated until his eyes were
almost entirely black. He leaned forward, his voice dropping to a rumble. "Now
*that* is the right question."

*Click.*

The bulb on the table turned blood red.

Michael didn't hesitate. He stood up. It hadn't been twenty minutes. He was
sure of it. But he didn't argue. He turned his back on Todd and walked out the
door before the room could be purged.
