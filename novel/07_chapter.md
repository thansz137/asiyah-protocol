# CHAPTER 7: The Watch

**Seventy-two hours earlier NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Maryland**

Eric Miller rubbed the grit from his eyes, the blue light of the telemetry
monitors burning into his retinas. Beside him, David Chen stared at his own
screen, tapping a rhythmic pattern on the desk that had been driving Miller
crazy since midnight. They were the sole occupants of the Flight Operations
room, a vast theater of empty chairs and darkened consoles.

The front wall dominated the room. A mosaic of projected maps and status bars
tracked the pulse of the orbital network. Hundreds of billions of dollars in
hardware drifting through the vacuum.

Third shift was maintenance work. While the western hemisphere slept, Miller
and Chen ran the diagnostic scripts that kept the US space assets talking to
the ground. It was usually a silent job, measured in the hum of cooling fans
and the taste of stale coffee.

The secure line on the console flashed crimson, shattering the calm. Miller
snatched the receiver. It was the Director of Operations. He didn't sound
groggy. He sounded cornered.

"Initiate a priority lockout on the Fermi telescope. Sever the public data
stream immediately. Then slew the instrument to these coordinates."

Miller typed the numbers into his terminal as the Director read them off. He
hesitated before hitting enter. "Sir? That slew rate is outside the flight
envelope. The guidance computer will flag it as a stability risk."

"Override the safety interlocks. Force the maneuver."

Miller looked at Chen. "Sir, if we override the safeties and the star trackers
lose lock, the bird dumps into Safe Mode. It'll shut down for twenty-four
hours. We'll go blind."

"I don't care about the risk. Move the telescope. Now."

The line went dead, though the indicator showed the Director was still
listening. Miller muted the microphone.

"Is he drunk?" Miller whispered.

"He sounds terrified," Chen said. He reached for his keyboard. "If we brick
this thing, it's my pension."

"Just do it. Bypass safeties. Execute."

The telemetry bars turned green. The slew was complete. The telescope was
staring into the dark.

"We are on station," Miller said. "Sensors are cold. Data stream active."

The Director didn't answer. He just breathed into the open line. A wet, hollow
static that filled the cavernous room. Miller and Chen sat frozen, eyes locked
on the waterfall display. Usually, gamma-ray detection was a slow trickle. A
photon here. A photon there. Painted onto the map over weeks.

The screen flooded white.

The readout didn't show the random scatter of a dying star or a black hole. It
showed rigid, repeating peaks. Hard geometry in the noise. Perfect intervals.

Chen stopped tapping his desk. He leaned in, nose inches from the glass. The
blood drained from his face. "That's impossible. That is a carrier wave."

Miller stared at the pattern. It was too clean. Too precise. "Director," he
croaked. "We have a signal lock. It's... it's artificial."

"Director?" Miller said. "Do you copy?"

The heavy breathing on the line stopped. There was a sharp click. The wet
static vanished, replaced by the absolute, digital silence of a prioritized
encryption channel.

When the response came, it wasn't the Director. The voice was calm. Measured.
Unmistakable.

"This is the President of the United States," the voice said. "Tell me exactly
what you are seeing."
