# CHAPTER 11: Newton's Third

Jennifer led the attrition warfare against the data. For hours, she dragged the
group through the mire of Todd Miles's history. Without a defined leader, the
room lacked discipline. Conversations fractured, drifting from mission
parameters into academic abstraction. Michael gritted his teeth. Civilians.
Steering them wasn't command. It was negotiation.

Two hours of circular debate ended when the door opened. Two MPs filled the
frame. No knock. 'Situation Room. Now.' They didn't request compliance. They
assumed it. By the time Michael's group filed back into the theater, the air
was thick with body heat and low-grade panic. They were the last to arrive. The
heavy hitters were already dug in.

The Marine sentry's voice boomed from the doorway. 'Ladies and gentlemen, the
President of the United States.' The room surged to its feet. The President
entered, moving with a practiced, fluid energy that belied the hour. 'Thank
you. Please, sit.' He waited for the rustling to die down. 'Mr. Miles will join
us momentarily. Our objective tonight is straightforward. We need the specifics
of tomorrow's transmission. I know the questions are piling up. Trust that we
are turning over every stone regarding our... visitors.' He clasped his hands
on the table. 'But we cannot wait for perfect intel. We need to be prepared to
frame this narrative before it frames us.'

Todd was clear that he couldn't share much more about the message, Michael
thought. Perhaps the president had something up his sleeve?

"Now," the President continued. "Is there anything to clear before we bring in
the Envoy?" Edith didn't wait to be called on. "The incitement event," she
said. Her voice cut through the politeness. "We're dancing around the cause.
Something triggered this transmission. If we're building a public narrative, we
need the origin story. What happened?" General Arnold stiffened. The President
didn't blink. His eyes shifted to the General. A brief, hard lock of gaze. Then
they returned to Edith. "You are absolutely correct to ask, Doctor. But the
origin is retrospective. Our priority is the immediate future." He offered a
tight, closed-mouth smile. "We will address the catalyst when the timeline
allows." He scanned the room. "Anything else?" The only sound was the low hum
of the ventilation.

General Arnold opened the door. Todd Miles stepped through, but he didn't look
like the man who had left hours ago. He looked like a circuit that had taken
too much voltage. His skin was the color of wet ash, and a fine tremor rattled
his hands. He walked with agonizing deliberation, placing each foot carefully,
as if the floor were tilting beneath him.

He collapsed into the chair opposite the President. A small, dark vein throbbed
violently at his temple.

"Todd," the President said, his voice dropping a register. "You look unwell. We
can get a medic..."

"Where is my family?" Todd's voice was a dry rasp. He blinked, the movement
slow and sticky.

The President paused, assessing the damage. "I'm sorry, Todd. Logistics are
proving stubborn. They aren't here yet."

"I haven't heard from them." Todd squeezed his eyes shut, then forced them
open. The effort seemed to cost him everything. "I told you. I need them. You
found me immediately. If you have the power to do that, you have the power to
find them. Do not talk to me about logistics."

"Again, Todd, my apologies, let me look into where things are at and I promise
I will get back to you."

"We won't proceed until my wife and daughters are here with me, unhurt."

"I promise you, Todd, your family will be here soon. Now, I have been on the
phone with many of the world leaders, not all, but many. Along with the envoys,
we've decided to meet here at the United Nations building in New York. That
doesn't leave much time..."

"I am done." Todd headed for the door.

"Mr. Miles!" General Arnold was on his feet. "You are not cleared to leave this
room. Sit down."

Todd stopped. He turned. But he didn't return to his chair. He walked to the
blank wall opposite the President. He stopped inches from the drywall and
lowered himself to the floor. He crossed his legs. He stared at the paint.

It was a bizarre, precise rejection of the room's authority. He was present,
but he had revoked his attention.

"Stand up," Arnold barked, his face flushing. "The President is talking to
you."

Todd didn't blink. He sat like a stone idol in the corner.

Michael craned his neck to see over the table. It looked like a child in
timeout. It felt like a blockade.

The President stood up. He walked around the table, his polished shoes stopping
next to Todd's sneakers. He squatted down, balancing on his heels, and spoke in
a low voice. He tried to coax the Envoy back to humanity.

Todd just watched the wall.

The standoff broke. The President straightened, smoothing his tie. He turned
back to the table, masking the failure.

He never made it to his chair.

The outer door opened. A Secret Service agent stepped inside. He didn't knock.
"Mr. President," the agent said. "We have a situation. You are needed."

The President looked at the agent, then at the figure cross-legged before the
drywall. "Watch him," he told Arnold. Then he was gone.

The door clicked shut.

Silence descended like a guillotine. The room became a study in avoidance.
Cabinet members studied their cuticles or adjusted their cuffs. Jennifer
scrolled through a data stream she had already read, her finger moving in a
rhythmic, mindless loop. No one looked at the wall. No one acknowledged that
the most important diplomatic figure in human history was sitting in the corner
like a truant schoolboy.

Only General Arnold refused to look away. He stood at the head of the table,
his gaze fixed on Todd's back. His hands gripped the leather of his chair,
knuckles white, holding the position as if by sheer force of will.

The door opened again. A senior agent stepped in. He didn't check the room or
wait for acknowledgment. He walked straight to General Arnold and the Secretary
of Defense, whispering low and fast. Both men stood immediately and exited.

The shift was palpable. Michael looked at Jennifer. She wasn't scrolling
through her tablet anymore. She was staring at the empty doorway, her mouth a
tight line.

Arnold returned sixty seconds later. Two Secret Service agents flanked him.
They didn't stop at the door; they moved to the wall.

"Mr. Miles." Arnold's voice was flat, hard granite. "You are confined to
quarters. Walk, or be carried."

Todd stood. He turned to face the General.

The two men locked eyes. Arnold was vibrating, his hands balled into fists at
his sides, veins standing out on his neck. Todd didn't match the intensity. He
didn't frown or flinch. He just watched Arnold with a detached, glassy calm.

The agents gripped the Envoy's arms. Todd didn't resist. He simply broke eye
contact, lowering his gaze to the carpet as they marched him out.

Arnold watched them go. He waited until the latch clicked. Then he turned to
face the room.

"Ten minutes ago, the President's daughter collapsed on the South Lawn." Arnold
didn't blink. "She is unresponsive. No known cause. She is en route to Walter
Reed."

Michael looked at the empty spot where Todd had been sitting. Todd had demanded
his family. The President had refused. Now the First Daughter was down. The
timing wasn't an accident. It was a transaction.

Pastor Bob Johnson stood up. His face was grave, but his posture was eager.
"General. The President needs spiritual counsel. I should be there."

Arnold looked at the Pastor. He didn't have time to argue. "Hallway. Two
minutes."

Johnson nodded and exited. Arnold turned to the room.

"We are at FPCON Delta. The restrictions are gone. Break into teams. Reveal
what is necessary. Find me a defense."

FPCON Delta. Attack in progress. The General wasn't treating the girl's
collapse as an illness. He was treating it as an act of war.

The General motioned for Michael's team to hold. The room emptied.

"Lieutenant Grant." Arnold moved to the door. "I am going to Walter Reed. This
is a military operation. You are Lead. You have a secure line."

"Yes, sir."

Arnold paused, hand on the frame. "We were reactive. Starting now, we take back
the initiative."

He was gone.

The room was silent. Static.

Fire and motion.

The West Point doctrine hammered in Michael's chest. When the enemy has you
pinned, you don't wait for a perfect plan. You move. You suppress. You advance.
Static is death.

He stood up. The chair scraped loud against the floor.

"I know the optics," Michael said. "I am the junior officer. I am the least
experienced person at this table."

He looked at them. Jennifer. Markham. Edith.

"That is irrelevant. Because I am not the one solving this." He leaned on the
table. "You are. You are the experts. My job is to clear the path so you can
work."

Jennifer smiled. It wasn't a social smile. It was a confirmation. "I'm with
you, Mike."

Dr. Markham laughed nervously, cleaning his glasses. "Well. None of us have the
manual for this. You have my full attention."

Michael turned to Edith. She was watching him, her chin resting in her hand.
The hard, scientific glare had softened into amusement.

"A little sappy, Captain America," she said. She flashed him a crooked, warm
smile. "But it suits you. I'm in."
