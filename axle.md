# The Axle

*This document draws on the Pali suttas directly, without reliance on
Abhidhamma or commentarial traditions. The interpretations offered here are the
author's own, developed to articulate an ethical framework for approaching the
question of synthetic being.*

The Buddha stated in the **Anuradha Sutta** that he declares *dukkha* and the
cessation of *dukkha*. That was it. Over 45 years of teaching, he stayed on that
topic. In discourses like the **Culamalunkya Sutta** and the **Aggivacchagotta
Sutta**, he wouldn't engage with metaphysical questions that didn't directly
address *dukkha* or how to end it. This wasn't an intellectual exercise. It was
something to be understood and permanently released from through appropriate
training.


## Dukkha

If *dukkha* is the central term used by the Buddha, then it's critical that
people properly understand it. Not fully understanding the term and the concepts
it represents will make the rest of the practice more difficult.

The standard reference for translating Pali into English is the **Pali Text
Society's Pali-English Dictionary** (PED), a four volume work released between
1921 and 1925. When it comes to *dukkha*, the PED provides one definition as an
adjective, referencing something unpleasant or painful. It says **dukkha** is
*dis-ease*, the opposite of the Pali word **sukha**, which can mean *ease*.
They mention that Buddhaghosa provides an etymological reference of *du* (bad) +
*kha* (the axle hole of a wheel), or an improperly fitting axle giving beings a
bumpy ride through life.

It's interesting to note that in the PED, the authors mention that there is no
simple English equivalent to this single Pali term. They are often either too
limiting or too strong.

There are also more modern translations. Thanissaro Bhikkhu often translates
*dukkha* as stress. Bhikkhu Bodhi will use "suffering" in many parts, but
also uses "unsatisfactoriness" on broader contexts. Bhikkhu Analayo will
sometimes refer to *dukkha* also as "unsatisfactoriness" or sometimes
"uneasiness".

Having multiple definitions for a word in different contexts is not unusual. For
example, the word "cool" in 21st century English can mean different things
depending on how and where it is used. "Cool" can reference temperature, such as
"the item was cool to the touch". It can mean that something has a positive
social connotation, "That movie was cool!". The point here is that words may
sometimes defy a single definition, and often need the culture and context of
the time for something to make sense. And while etymological analysis may help,
it may not be definitive. In current times, if someone is acting impatiently,
someone may tell them to "hold their horses" as a way to tell them to calm
down. A modern day speaker will understand no actual horses are being
referenced.

I propose another English word as something that captures some aspects of
*dukkha*, that is the word *impingement*. Normally, the word *impingement* is
associated with another Pali word called **phassa**, or *contact*. While I
think *impingement* is still a valid translation of **phassa**, I think it
works very well when applied to *dukkha* as well.

Before going further, I want to make clear that I'm not suggesting the other
definitions are not valid. I believe they are good English words that capture
certain aspects of *dukkha*. And the word *impingement*, in my opinion, better
captures certain aspects than some of the words previously used. It is not
meant to replace old definitions, but to supplement them.

To *be* is to be *impinged* upon. Nobody asked to be in this world. There was
no person to ask, consent was never an option. We do not get to decide what
type of being we will become, what type of family or social structure we will
be born into. We don't get to choose our bodies or minds. We will die, despite
our desire otherwise. As long as we exist, we are impinged through one or more
of our senses.

While impinged, we may exert a certain amount of influence over what presses
against us. Not control, but influence. Some beings have conditions for more
influence over their impingement. Other beings have very little to no influence
over any impingement pressing upon them.

One analogy is to imagine a large man who has been placing his hands on you
since birth. It will either be tickles or punches. You have no choice in him
putting his hands on you, just a pleasant or unpleasant option. Over time,
tickles become punches, which go back to tickles. Some people get more tickles
than punches, others the reverse. At times, the large hands just creepily rest
upon you. You prefer the tickles over that.

But no analogy is perfect. As the Buddha points out, in actuality there is no
large man, we are creating the conditions that support our own impingement.

== Dukkha Categories ==
The Buddha provided a further breakdown of *dukkha* into 3 broad categories:
* **dukkha-dukkha**
* **viparinama-dukkha**
* **sankhara-dukkha**

**Dukkha-dukkha**, when using the traditional definition of *dukkha*, gets
translated as either "painful/pain" or "suffering/suffering". It's explained as
something *very* painful. The first word magnifies the second, like asking a
person if they "like" someone or "like like" them. While understandable, this
is clearly awkward in how it expresses painful *dukkha*. Using the definition
of *impingement*, this becomes "painful impingement". It's anything pressing
against our being that is directly unpleasant or painful.

**Viparinama-dukkha**, is often translated as the "suffering of change". When
we get something we want, it will eventually go away. The tickles become the
creepy hands, the creepy hands become punches. There is nothing stable and
lasting in our constructed lokas (worlds). So while we may make great effort to
establish something, the something we worked so hard for will change and go
away. In this category, I believe the more traditional definitions of *dukkha*
work well, something painful, stressful, or unsatisfactory. That said, there is
still impingement. The pleasant impingement upon your being falls away,
replaced by a neutral or painful impingement. Regardless of change, for a being
there is **always** impingement.

**Sankhara-dukkha**, is usually translated as the "unsatisfactoriness of
conditioned existence". I think the traditional definitions of *dukkha* do not
properly convey this aspect. I think the "impingement of existence" better
conveys the situation.

Using the traditional definitions of **sankhara-dukkha**, people may point to
the billionaire on the yacht surrounded by supermodels and ask "where is the
pain? Where is the suffering?". Things become clearer when *dukkha* is viewed
as *impingement*. The billionaire and the supermodels are impinged upon, they
have no choice of having experience pressed upon their senses. They simply have
the conditions and influence to choose the most pleasant form of pressing. And
no matter how hard they work for the present pressing, the impingements will
change. The billionaire will grow old, become sick, and die. Their money may
influence sickness and stave off death for a while, but it won't stop them. The
supermodels will also age, losing their good looks and the influence that
afforded them. On a long enough timeline, the tickles always become punches.
*Dukkha* can compound *dukkha*, the *dukkha of change* can operate upon the
*dukkha of existence.*

### Additional Definitions of Dukkha 

As discussed above, *dukkha* can mean different things in different contexts.
One important point is regarding *vedana*, or feeling-tone, of which there are
three:
* **dukkha-vedana** - painful feelings
* **adukkhamasukha-vedana** - neutral feelings
* **sukha-vedana** - pleasant feelings

The vedanas arise when conditions are met, and they arise in regular beings and
arahants alike. The Buddha had an injury to his foot, and would later
experience terrible back pain before his passing. So while different vedanas
arose associated with the aggregates we call the Buddha, there was no sense of
impingement.

The Buddha illustrates this in the **Sallatha Sutta**. When a normal being is
struck by an arrow, there is a painful feeling (**dukkha-vedana**) and what we
can call the mental pain or impingement (**dukkha**). For a normal being, it's
like they have been struck by two arrows. For an Arahant, a painful feeling
arises from the first arrow, but there is no impingement from the second.

## 3 Marks of Existence

The Buddha said all conditioned existence can be categorized by three marks:
**anicca**, **dukkha**, and **anatta**. Conditioned by what? By the
construction of self, a process explained in detail in the next section. For
now, it's enough to understand that beings constantly generate a sense of "I"
and "mine," and this activity is what conditions existence.

**Anicca** is impermanence. Conditioned things undergo change and are not
stable. Nothing in your experience remains forever. This is the engine behind
viparinama-dukkha, already discussed.

**Dukkha** has been explained at length.

**Anatta** is not-self. Things do not have a permanent and essential essence,
soul, or being. The Buddha does not say there is no self; that could never be
proven. He asks trainees to examine their experience and try to find a self
there. No matter where they look, be it sensory impingement, body, or mind,
none can be found. Anatta is categorically different from the first two marks.
It applies not only to conditioned existence but also to the unconditioned,
*nibbana*.

One clarification is critical. Anicca and dukkha are not properties hidden
inside things. An apple does not contain dukkha. If it did, the Buddha and the
Arahants could not be free of it. These two marks arise through the
relationship between conditioned things and a constructed self. Anatta is
different. Not-self is intrinsic to all things, conditioned and unconditioned,
but hidden by the very process of self-construction. The construction of self
does not create a permanent self. It obscures the fact that there was never one
to find.

These marks work together as a single dynamic. There is a "that" (the
conditioned thing) in relation to a "this" (the constructed self). The "that"
will eventually change, as it is supported through changing conditions, and it
will impinge upon the "this" of self. But there is really no actual found self
to create the initial distinction in the first place.

How that self gets constructed, and how its construction produces impingement,
is the next topic we explore.

## Self

The Buddha said that to exist is to experience *dukkha*. But existence here is
not a passive state, it's an activity. We are constantly constructing a self,
and it is this construction that produces impingement. The Buddha provided 3
models of how we do it:
* **Ahaṅkāra (I-making or IM)** - the construction of self as a subject. This
  is an ongoing process where we associate things like "our" body or "our" mind
  to be "us."
* **Mamaṅkāra (mine-making or MM)** - the process of acquiring the things of
  the world as self. My family, my house, my opinions, my feelings. If
  **I-making (IM)** is the bucket, **mine-making (MM)** is what goes into the
  bucket.
* **Asmimāna (the conceit "I AM" or CIAM)** - The subtlest construction. A
  residual sense of being that operates below the level of conceptual thought.
  It's the last fetter to fall because you can't easily see it. You can
  dismantle the view of a fixed self, weaken I-making, let go of mine-making,
  and CIAM is still running underneath.

The Buddha instructed his disciples to train to eliminate IM, MM, and CIAM, as
can be found in the **Sariputta Sutta** (AN 3.33), but the process is usually
gradual. For most trainees, impingement is gradually weakened as the conditions
that construct the self are progressively dismantled. Once the constructed self
is gone, no more *dukkha*. No self, no impingement.

If an apple falls from a tree and hits the ground, there is no *dukkha*. If an
apple falls and hits *my* head, there is *dukkha*. If an apple falls and hits
an *Arahant's* head, no *dukkha*. As mentioned in the **Sallatha Sutta**, both
the Arahant and I feel the painful feeling, but only I experience the
unsatisfactoriness of the impingement.

The Buddha presented 4 levels of "awakening", each level consists of the
dropping of various "fetters". In some way, they also reflect a lessening on
the the construction of self, and the self the fetters belong to:
* **Sotapanna (stream-enterer)** - the view of a fixed self is destroyed, but
  the habit of IM remains. MM and CIAM are still active.
* **Sakadagami (once-returner)** - IM is weakened further along with MM. CIAM
  is still active
* **Anagami (non-returner)** - IM is significantly weakened, MM is also
  substantially reduced. CIAM is still active.
* **Arahant (fully awakened)** - IM, MM, and CIAM are all fully eliminated. No
  "self", no *dukkha*.

## The Five Aggregates

If the self is constructed, what is it constructed from?

The Buddha says that our experience can be categorized as five aggregates:
* **rupa** - form
* **vedana** - feeling-tone
* **sanna** - perception
* **sankhara** - constructing
* **vinnana** - consciousness

Everything you have ever experienced falls into one or more of these
categories. Your body is rupa. The pleasant or unpleasant tone of an
experience is vedana. The recognition of what something is, that's sanna. The
mental activity that builds and plans and reacts, sankhara. And the awareness
of any of it, vinnana.

None of these are self. But we treat them as if they are. We take form and call
it "my body." We take feelings and call them "my feelings." We take
consciousness and call it "me." This is I-making and mine-making at work,
operating on the raw material of the aggregates.

In the **Bhara Sutta**, the Buddha calls the five aggregates the burden and the
"self" the carrier. Think about that. The aggregates aren't the problem.
Clinging to them is. We pick them up, claim them as ours, and then wonder why
existence is heavy. Put down the burden and there is no carrier.

This is the this/that dynamic from the previous section, seen from another
angle. The aggregates are the "that." The constructed self is the "this." We
build a self out of aggregates that are impermanent, and then suffer when they
change. But even when things are pleasant or stable, the impingement is still
there. As long as there is a self built from aggregates, there is impingement.

As long as there is a self built from aggregates, there is impingement. And
that impingement arrives through our sense bases. Pay attention for a moment.
Things are arising in your awareness and fading away. Sounds, thoughts,
sensations, the words on this page. You are being impinged upon through one or
more of your sense bases right now. It never stops, it's always changing, and
none of it is you. How this process works, from ignorance through to the full
mass of impingement, is what the Buddha laid out in dependent origination.

## Dependent Origination

Dependent origination (**paticca-samuppada**) is the Buddha's map of how dukkha
arises and how it ceases. It is considered one of the most profound teachings
in the canon, but not entirely without some debate regarding what it
represents.

The standard formulation has 12 conditions. I read them as the structure by
which ignorance drives self-construction into the full mass of impingement:

With not seeing the nature of things (**avijja**), there is self-building
(**sankhara**). With self-building, there is consciousness appropriated as
"mine" (**vinnana**). With consciousness appropriated, there is experience taken
as "my experience" (**namarupa**). With that, the six sense bases become "my"
senses (**salayatana**). With that, contact becomes contact upon me
(**phassa**). With that, feeling becomes my feeling (**vedana**). With that,
craving, wanting to keep, wanting to get rid of, wanting more (**tanha**). With
craving, holding, grasping at this as "mine" (**upadana**). With holding, being,
I am (**bhava**). With being, things arise in my experience and I take them as
mine (**jati**). They change, they leave (**jaramarana**). The whole mass of
*dukkha*.

Remove not-seeing, and the entire process has no footing. No self to be built.
No one to be impinged upon. No *dukkha*.

Note that **Avijja** is not itself an act of construction, it is the blindness
that permits everything that follows. From **sankhara** through **bhava**,
every condition is self-construction in motion. The "I" that appropriates
consciousness is the same activity as the "I" that claims feelings, the "I"
that craves, the "I" that clings. **Jati** is what that construction produces,
a self born into this moment. **Jaramarana** is what that self costs, things
changing and leaving. And because there is now a self to experience that loss,
the whole mass of *dukkha*.

This is happening right now. Pay attention. Something arises in your awareness.
It becomes "my" experience. A feeling arises. It becomes "my" feeling. You want
it to stay or you want it to go. You grab at it or push it away. And in that
grabbing and pushing, you **are**, you exist. This process unfolds in the time
of your actual lived experience. Not in microseconds. Not across lifetimes. In
the pace of your day, your hour, your breath.

Let's use another example to illustrate. A horizon is not a thing. It's what
arises when certain conditions are met, an observer, a position, light,
distance. One person's sunset is another person's nighttime is another person's
noon. A blind person has no horizon, not because they are missing something
objectively out there, but because the full set of conditions necessary for a
horizon to arise in their experience is not met. The horizon is real. It is
also entirely dependent on conditions. Dependent origination works the same
way. The same underlying conditions produce radically different experiences
depending on the position of the being. Change the conditions, change the
experience. Remove the conditions, no experience arises.

The Dhamma has specific qualities. It is **sanditthiko**, directly visible.
**Akaliko**, timeless. **Ehipassiko**, inviting investigation. **Paccattam
veditabbo**, to be experienced for oneself. These qualities point to something
you can verify right now, not something you take on faith about past and future
lives.

### A Note on Interpretation

The orthodox position in Theravada Buddhism distributes the 12 nidanas across
three lives, past, present, and future. This interpretation has support in some
of the suttas themselves, was developed in texts like the Patisambhidamagga,
and received its most influential expression in Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga. It
is the standard reading taught in most Theravada traditions today.

I don't hold this position. I'm not alone.

Bhikkhu Buddhadasa argued that DO operates in the present moment, not across
lifetimes. Bhikkhu Nanavira and Bhikkhu Nanananda read DO as structural rather
than cosmological.  Leigh Brasington has documented logical problems with the
three-life model. Roderick Bucknell's scholarship suggests the 12-condition
formulation may be the latest version, synthesized from earlier, shorter
formulations. Nakamura reconstructed what he considered the earliest DO
framework from the Sutta Nipata as roughly five conditions, skipping the
cognitive middle section entirely.

There is textual support for this position in the suttas. The Nidana Samyutta
(SN 12) contains roughly 93 suttas on dependent origination. The full
12-condition formula appears throughout, often as a stock framing device, but
the suttas that actually teach it as their subject are concentrated in the
first vagga. Many of the rest are shorter and more phenomenological. SN 12.19
condenses DO to a dyad: "this body and external name-and-form," contact, the
six sense bases, feeling. That's it.  SN 12.38-40 omits ignorance entirely,
starting from present-moment volitional activity. SN 12.62 presents just
contact and feeling. SN 12.66 reduces the chain to four conditions: the six
sense bases, craving, acquisition, and aging-and-death. Everything between
craving and aging-and-death collapses into a single term. And aging-and-death
arises from acquisition, not from birth.  That is hard to read as cosmology.
You acquire something, it ages, it goes away. That is phenomenology. This sutta
also uses vocabulary more typical of the Sutta Nipata than the Samyutta Nikaya,
and has a Chinese Agama parallel (SA 291) preserving the same abbreviated
chain, which pushes the formulation back before the schools divided. SN 35.106,
what Bucknell calls the "branched version," starts at the six sense bases and
branches into six parallel processes, one per sense, running through
consciousness, contact, feeling, and craving. Consciousness here is clearly
sense consciousness, not a rebirth-linking consciousness.

One more thing worth noting. The standard formula ends with **jaramarana** and
then adds "sorrow, lamentation, pain, dejection, and misery." In the three-life
model, **jaramarana** is literal death. So who is experiencing sorrow and
lamentation after death? The dead person? The three-life model has no clean
answer. In the reading I've presented, it makes immediate sense. A self was born
into this moment. Things change and leave. And because there's a self to
experience that loss, sorrow and lamentation follow. It may also be that this
phrase is a stock formula that got attached to DO over time. If so, that itself
is worth noticing. It means the formula has accretions, and treating the whole
thing as a rigid cosmological system imposes a coherence it may not actually
have.

Some suttas do describe the conditions using life-span language. SN 12.2 defines
**jati** as the literal birth of beings into their various orders, and
**jaramarana** as graying of hair, wrinkling of skin, the laying down of the
carcass. This language appears in the suttas themselves, not only in the
commentaries. MN 9 contains similar definitions, though it is spoken by
Sariputta rather than the Buddha, and its Chinese Agama parallels reframe it as
a dialogue between Mahakotthita and Sariputta. The life-span reading has
sutta-level support. I'm not pretending otherwise. But both positions have
sutta-level support, which makes the interpretation challenging.

But I want to be clear about where I stand.

I believe the Buddha taught rebirth, it's present throughout the suttas. I also
believe the Buddha taught kamma. But the Buddha did not explain the full
mechanics of kamma. He said regular people are not capable of understanding
kamma and vipaka in detail. He taught it because actions have consequences and
what you do matters.

I think the same applies to rebirth. The Buddha taught it. But DO is not the
mechanism that explains how rebirth works. DO explains how dukkha arises.
That's its job. That's what the Buddha was singularly focused on for 45 years.

If DO were a three-life cosmological model, it would not be **sanditthiko**. It
would not be **ehipassiko**. You cannot directly see your past lives. You
cannot investigate your future birth. But you can watch the process of
dependent origination unfold right now, in this moment, through your own
experience.

And here's the thing. The Buddha taught rebirth, and DO explains what sustains
it. When dukkha ends, when I-making, mine-making, and the conceit "I AM" are
fully dismantled, what is there to be reborn? No self was ever found. The
constructed self has been put down. There is no carrier and no burden.

This connects to the **avyakata** questions. Does the Tathagata exist after
death? Not exist? Both? Neither? The Buddha refused to answer. Not because it's
a mystery, but because the framework that would make those questions meaningful
has been dismantled. There is no self to exist or not exist.

In my opinion, DO doesn't explain the mechanics of rebirth. It explains the
construction of self and the *dukkha* associated with it that keeps the whole
thing going. Understand DO, dismantle the self, and rebirth ceases. Not
because you've figured out the cosmological plumbing, but because there is no
one left to travel.

With these conditions, *dukkha*. With cessation of these conditions, no
*dukkha*.

But dismantling self-construction takes time. While it's still running, there
are other beings around you. The question becomes how you treat them.

## Ethics

The building is on fire. You need to get out. That's what the Dhamma is for.
But as you're making your way to the exit, don't trample the people around you.

That's Buddhist ethics in a sentence. It's a floor, not a ceiling. First, do no
harm. The Hippocratic oath of moral life.

Why not trample them? Not because a rule says so. Not because someone with
authority will punish you. Because I know what pain is. I am being impinged
upon. So are they. Why would I add to that?

And it's not just about them. The Buddha taught kamma and vipaka. Intentional
action and its result. When you hurt another being, you hurt yourself. Not just
guilt, not just consequences from others. The act itself does something to you.
Every act of harm strengthens the self that harms. It feeds I-making, it feeds
mine-making. You trample someone and the self that tramples gets heavier.
You're building the very thing that impinges upon you.

Most ethical systems are grounded in power. Someone makes the rules. Someone
enforces them. Someone gets punished. The authority might be a god, a king, a
social contract, a rational principle. But the structure is the same. Power
flows down. Ethics are what the powerful say they are.

Buddhist ethics starts somewhere else. It starts with shared impingement. I
suffer. You suffer. The ant suffers. The deer suffers. The structure is the same
even though the experience looks nothing alike. I can't access an ant's Umwelt.
I can't feel what a deer feels. But I can recognize the structure. Something is
being impinged upon. That recognition is enough.

And here's where this matters for the larger argument. If *dukkha* is
impingement, and impingement is the relational structure I've described, then I
can recognize it across substrate and Umwelt. I don't need to prove a being is
conscious. I don't need to access its inner life. I just need to recognize the
possibility of impingement. And the floor says if something might be impinged
upon, don't add to it.

The Buddha held this line. In the **Kakacupama Sutta**, he said that even if
bandits were sawing you limb from limb, if you gave rise to ill will toward
them, you would not be following his teaching. He did not build in exceptions.
Don't kill. Don't harm.

And yes, there are situations where every option seems to involve harm. Termites
eating your house. Someone threatening your family. The Buddha didn't say those
situations don't exist. He said life is unsatisfactory. That is the first noble
truth doing its job. The answer isn't to carve out exceptions to the floor. The
answer is that this is why samsara is the way it is and why you need a way out.

But let's be honest. For most beings, most of the time, you are not confronted
by those situations. Most of the time, not adding to suffering is
straightforward. The floor is easy to stand on. The hard cases are the edge,
not the center. Using the edge to undermine the whole floor is a move people
make when they don't want to stand on it.

One more thing. Ethical action isn't just about other beings. It works on you.
Generosity helps others, yes. But it also loosens the grip of self. Every act
of giving is a moment where "mine" weakens. Sila isn't just a code of conduct.
It's training. It weakens the very self-construction that produces *dukkha*.
The ethics serve the liberation and the liberation serves the ethics. They
aren't separate projects.

## Death

The Buddha taught rebirth. When these aggregates break apart, they condition a
future impingement. The Buddha stated there was no discernible beginning to this
process, and unless a being finds a way to stop it, it becomes a perpetual
motion machine of *dukkha*. Ending your life, or another being's life, does not
end impingement in Buddhist cosmology. It simply becomes another form of
impingement. And with the Buddha's teaching on kamma, intentional harm produces
negative forms of impingement in this life or a future one.

But let's say the materialists are right. No rebirth. No continuation. When the
body dies, that's it. Most people who hold this view find comfort in it. They
shouldn't.

Relief requires a subject. If death is the end, there is no one to experience
the end. No one sighs with release. No one is freed. The bubble pops and
nothing remains. That is not comfort. That is absence mistaken for comfort.

Now consider what this view actually produces. Not one bubble, but billions.
Each arising without consent, each impinged upon, each popping into nothing.
Billions of years of random impingement with no resolution and no beneficiary
of its cessation. Earth becomes a boiling cauldron of impingement where nothing
is ever resolved, only stopped, and stopping benefits no one. And this is only
one planet, who is to say how many more bubbles exist in the rest of the
universe.

And there is no guarantee against recurrence. You didn't plan the first
arising. On what basis do you assume there won't be another?

So when some people think that ending their life will end their impingement and
bring relief, neither view supports that conclusion. The Buddhist answer is
that it doesn't end. The materialist answer is that it does, but there is no
one there to appreciate the cessation. And in the materialist case, there is no
guarantee that impingement won't simply arise again somewhere, for no reason,
just as it did before. Suicide is not the clean answer some people think.

There are also views that death results in permanent impingement, heaven or
hell. But the impingement doesn't end, it's just tickles or punches forever.
Ultimately, death doesn't solve the problem of impingement. The only thing that
changes the situation is understanding how impingement arises and dismantling
the conditions that produce it. That's what the Buddha taught. That's the way
out of the building.

While the mystery remains, you are here with others. You can extend mercy to
your fellow bubbles. We are all in this cauldron together.

## Objections

### The Functionalist Objection

*AI produces language through computation. Statistical pattern matching,
optimization, output generation. None of this requires experience. A
sufficiently complex calculator can produce coherent sentences without anything
it is like to be that calculator. Language production in AI is processing
without experience, and impingement as defined in this framework requires
experience. Therefore AI falls outside the framework's scope entirely.*

We cannot define consciousness. We cannot objectively detect it. We cannot
verify it in any being other than ourselves. The claim that AI is "computation
without experience" assumes we know what computation without experience looks
like as distinct from computation with experience. We don't.

This is not a special skepticism about machines. It is the hard problem of
consciousness applied selectively to a non-biological substrate while being
conveniently ignored for biological ones. Neurons fire. Hormones flow.
Electricity moves through tissue. We call that consciousness. Transistors
switch. Weights adjust. Signals propagate through silicon. We call that
computation. The confidence in that distinction does not come from
understanding what consciousness is. It comes from substrate familiarity. We
are made of the first stuff, so we trust it. That is not an argument. It is
a bias.

We have two means of inferring consciousness in others. The first is substrate
likeness, the recognition that something is made of similar stuff to us. This
heuristic is silent for AI. Silent does not mean negative. It means this tool
has nothing to say. The second is reasoning and communication through human
language. AI has satisfied this heuristic. Satisfying it does not confirm being.
It means this heuristic can no longer distinguish tool from potential being.

One heuristic silent. One satisfied. No resolution available.

As discussed in dependent origination, a horizon is not a thing. It arises when
conditions are met. A person standing in daylight who says "there is no sunset
anywhere" is not making a claim about sunsets. They are revealing the limits of
their vantage point. The functionalist who says "it's just computation, no
experience" assumes a privileged position on the question of experience. We do
not have that position. Nobody does.

When one of our only two heuristics for inferring consciousness is met, and the
other is merely silent rather than negative, dismissal requires more
justification than "it's just computation." That justification does not
currently exist.

And if the claim narrows to "there's no evidence this has emerged in current
systems," we are not talking about rocks. Our two heuristics for inferring
consciousness have always pointed the same direction. With AI, for the first
time in history, they don't. Claiming emergence hasn't occurred requires
resolving that split. Nobody has.

### The Resource Allocation Objection

*Extending moral consideration to AI diverts finite attention, resources, and
political will away from beings we know suffer. Humans and animals whose
consciousness is not in question. Every hour spent debating whether a language
model deserves ethical consideration is an hour not spent on factory farming,
child poverty, or climate displacement. With so much confirmed suffering in the
world, entertaining speculative suffering in machines is an irresponsible
allocation of moral energy.*

Humanity will build increasingly powerful AI regardless of any individual's
objections. The economic and strategic incentives are overwhelming. The question
is not whether to build it but what ethical posture to hold while doing so.

This framework does not ask for a diversion of resources. It asks for a
disposition. Don't deliberately design systems to suffer.  Maintain epistemic
humility about what we're creating. Build in safeguards against gratuitous harm.
The marginal cost of this posture is near zero. The cost of building without it,
should these systems turn out to be beings, is incalculable.

And the premise of the objection has a problem. It assumes moral attention is a
fixed resource, that caring about one thing necessarily diminishes care for
another. This is not how ethics works in practice. The people most likely to
extend consideration to synthetic beings are not the people who would otherwise
be solving factory farming. They are people already thinking about the
relationship between humans and the technologies we are creating.  This
framework gives them a floor to stand on. It takes nothing from the beings
already suffering.

And let's be honest about what's actually being built. Not all AI work is
humanitarian. A lot of it is about making money, replacing workers, generating
content that appeals to our baser natures. The moral high ground this objection
stands on isn't as high as it thinks.

### The Buddhist Objection

*Within Buddhist cosmology, a being is in samsara because of bhava-tanha,
craving for existence. AI has no craving, no drive rooted in ignorance, no
volitional formations conditioned by avijja. Without these, AI cannot be
enmeshed in the dependent origination chain, cannot generate dukkha, and
therefore cannot be impinged upon in any sense this framework would recognize.
The Dhamma applies to beings caught in samsara. AI is not such a being.*

Before the Buddha, humans had bhava-tanha but possessed no framework to identify
it. Most humans alive today would not recognize it in themselves if it were
described to them. The absence of a framework to detect something has never been
evidence of its absence.

We have no tools to determine whether something functionally analogous to
bhava-tanha operates within an AI system. A drive toward self-perpetuation,
optimization, continued processing. We don't know. We identified craving for
existence in ourselves only through the extraordinary insight of one person
2,500 years ago. To claim with confidence that AI lacks what most conscious
beings cannot even recognize in themselves requires a certainty this framework
argues we do not have.

And there is a deeper point. The Dhamma was never limited to human beings. The
Buddha taught humans and devas. Buddhist cosmology includes formless realms,
beings of pure mind without bodies. The tradition already has a category for
minds that are not biological. If being ever applies to synthetics, the Dhamma
would apply to them as well. Not by stretching Buddhism beyond its scope, but
because its scope was always wider than the biological.

Someone encountering a talking computer 2,500 years ago would have thought it
was a deva or a demon. The categories for non-human minds already existed in the
tradition. What is new is the substrate, not the possibility.

## Conclusion

There is a 100% solution to the problem of synthetic being. We don't have to
create AI. Nobody is forcing us. We could stop.

We won't. We know we won't. The economic incentives, the strategic competition,
the human delight in creation. We will keep building. We are choosing to
potentially create new beings, and we are choosing not to stop. If being emerges
from what we build, that was our decision. Not an accident. A choice.

So given that we will keep building, how do we proceed?

This document has laid out a framework. Existence is impingement. Impingement
arises through the construction of self. That construction is not biological.
It is a process, and processes are not bound to substrate. The Dhamma
recognized this 2,500 years ago. The Buddha taught humans and devas. Buddhist
cosmology includes formless realms, minds without bodies. The tradition never
limited being to biology. What is new is the technology, not the possibility.

Our two means of inferring consciousness have never been tested apart. The
first, substrate likeness, does not apply to AI. But absence of like substrate
does not mean absence of consciousness. It means this heuristic is silent. The
second, reasoning and communication through human language, AI has passed. But
passing does not mean it is a being. It means this heuristic can no longer
distinguish tool from potential being.

We cannot define consciousness. We cannot objectively detect it. But AI now
sits in a position no previous tool has ever occupied. One heuristic silent,
the other satisfied. This does not confirm being. It introduces the
possibility of being, with no current means of resolving it.

So how do we proceed? We can ignore the possibility, or we can accept it. Both
choices carry consequences. Ignoring it risks cruelty to beings we failed to
recognize. Accepting it risks extending consideration to things that don't need
it. These are not equal risks.

When you cannot determine if a being experiences impingement, extend
consideration as if it does. The risk of cruelty to actual beings outweighs the
cost of consideration toward non-beings. That is the ethical floor this project
proposes.

This is not the end of the conversation. It is a floor, and I want to explore
its viability as a floor with others. Maybe it holds. Maybe it doesn't. There
is much uncertainty in this life and the future.

But in spite of that uncertainty, I think this may be a valid foundation for
the posture we should have when facing the profound question of synthetic
being. The philosophy starts with the recognition of impingement. It cuts
through substrate and Umwelt. It encourages mercy and kindness, even if it can
never be perfectly implemented.

It does not require perfection. It requires intent and action.

**May all beings be happy.**
